# Rome Statute of the International Criminal Court 1998 (ICC)

The Rome Statute of the International Criminal Court 1998 (popularly the Rome Statute / formally Rome Statute of the International Criminal Court) is a global supranational meta-framework international-criminal-court binding-treaty instrument adopted by the UN Diplomatic Conference of Plenipotentiaries — structurally distinctive globally as the only modern universal multilateral binding-treaty framework establishing a permanent International Criminal Court with formal jurisdiction over war crimes + crimes against humanity + genocide + aggression (Rome Statute adopted 17 July 1998 by UN Diplomatic Conference of Plenipotentiaries on the Establishment of an International Criminal Court in Rome + effective 1 July 2002 — formal universal multilateral binding-treaty framework establishing permanent International Criminal Court ICC with formal complementarity-to-national-jurisdiction framework, distinct from rest-of-modern-international-criminal-court frameworks which operate as ad hoc tribunals like ICTY + ICTR + Special Tribunals, structurally distinctive globally as the only modern universal binding-treaty framework establishing permanent international criminal court), as the central jurisdiction of the formal Article 8 war-crimes-and-children framework (Rome Statute Article 8(2)(b)(xxvi) framework providing 'Conscripting or enlisting children under the age of fifteen years into the national armed forces or using them to participate actively in hostilities' as a war crime + Article 8(2)(e)(vii) framework providing same for non-international armed conflicts + Article 7(1)(g) framework providing 'sexual slavery, enforced prostitution, forced pregnancy, enforced sterilization or any other form of sexual violence of comparable gravity' as a crime against humanity — formal foundational war-crimes-and-children + crimes-against-humanity-and-sexual-violence framework, structurally distinctive globally as the only modern universal binding-treaty framework operating formal war-crimes-and-children framework, with substantial relevance to PA framework via formal cross-border-child-soldiers framework + cross-border-child-victims-of-armed-conflict framework), as the central jurisdiction of the formal Article 17 complementarity framework (Rome Statute Article 17 framework providing complementarity-to-national-jurisdiction framework where ICC only has jurisdiction when state is 'unwilling or unable genuinely to carry out the investigation or prosecution' — formal foundational complementarity framework, structurally distinctive globally as the only modern universal binding-treaty framework operating formal complementarity-to-national-jurisdiction framework), and as the central jurisdiction of the formal Lubanga 2012 + Bemba 2016 + Ongwen 2021 ICC case-law framework on child-victims-of-armed-conflict (ICC Prosecutor v Thomas Lubanga Dyilo Judgment 14 March 2012 + Prosecutor v Jean-Pierre Bemba Gombo Judgment 21 March 2016 + Prosecutor v Dominic Ongwen Judgment 4 February 2021 — formal foundational ICC case-law framework on child-victims-of-armed-conflict, structurally distinctive globally as the most-developed modern international-criminal-court case-law framework on child-victims-of-armed-conflict framework). The Rome Statute is binding upon 124 state parties as of 2026 — substantial state-party coverage framework with significant non-state-party framework including United States + Russia + China + India + Israel + others. The Statute is silent on 'parental alienation' as a statutory label, though formal Article 7(1)(g) crimes-against-humanity-and-sexual-violence + Article 8(2)(b)(xxvi) + Article 8(2)(e)(vii) war-crimes-and-children frameworks operate substantial intersection with parental-alienation framework via formal cross-border-child-soldiers framework + cross-border-child-victims-of-armed-conflict framework.

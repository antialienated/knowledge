## The Legal Standard: Both Parents Have Equal Rights {#legal-standard}

Unless a court order **specifically** restricts one parent's access to medical and mental health records, **both parents with legal custody have equal rights** [to](https://www.apa.org/topics/divorce-child-custody):

- All treatment information
- Diagnosis and treatment plans
- Session summaries and progress notes
- Communication with the therapist
- Participation in treatment decisions

This is not a courtesy. This is **federal law** (HIPAA), **state law** (in all 50 U.S. states), and **professional ethics codes** (APA, NASW, AAMFT).

> 💡 **Pro Insight:** The phrase you need is: *"I have joint legal custody and I am requesting access to my child's treatment information under HIPAA and [your state] family code."* Put it in writing. Every time.

The APA Ethics Code, Standard 3.10, requires psychologists to obtain informed consent from all parties with legal authority over the child. The NASW Code of Ethics similarly mandates that social workers respect the rights of all parties involved in a minor's treatment.

## What the Therapist Must Provide to Both Parents {#must-provide}

Here is the **minimum** that a competent, ethical child therapist must provide to each parent with legal custody:

### 1. Diagnosis (If Any)
You have the right to know if your child has been diagnosed with any condition—anxiety, depression, adjustment disorder, PTSD, or any DSM-5 classification.

**Acceptable:** "Your child meets criteria for Adjustment Disorder with Mixed Disturbance of Emotions and Conduct (F43.25), which we're addressing through CBT."

**Unacceptable:** "I can't share that with you" or "Ask your co-parent."

### 2. Treatment Plan and Goals
What is the therapist working on? What modality are they using? What are the measurable objectives?

**Acceptable:** "We're using play therapy to help [child] process the transition between homes. Our goals are reducing anxiety at exchanges and improving emotional vocabulary."

**Unacceptable:** "We're just talking" or "It's confidential between me and the child."

### 3. Progress Updates
How is the child doing? Are they improving? What benchmarks are being used?

**Acceptable:** "Over the past 6 sessions, [child]'s anxiety scores on the SCARED assessment have decreased from 28 to 19. She's using coping strategies more consistently."

**Unacceptable:** "She's doing fine" with no specifics, or refusing to provide updates entirely.

### 4. Session Frequency and Format
How often are sessions? Individual or family? Are both parents included in family sessions?

**Acceptable:** "We meet weekly on Thursdays. I'd like to schedule a parent session with each of you individually, and then a joint session if appropriate."

**Unacceptable:** "I only meet with [other parent] for parent sessions" or scheduling all sessions during one parent's custody time.

### 5. Crisis Information
If the child expresses suicidal ideation, self-harm, or reports abuse, both parents must be notified.

**Acceptable:** Immediate notification to both parents with documentation.

**Unacceptable:** Only notifying the parent who brought the child to that session.

### 6. Recommendations
Any recommendations for medication, additional evaluations, school accommodations, or changes to the parenting plan.

**Acceptable:** "I'm recommending a psychoeducational evaluation through the school district and would like both parents' consent for the referral."

**Unacceptable:** Making recommendations to one parent only or coordinating changes without both parents' knowledge.

## What Acceptable Answers Sound Like {#acceptable-answers}

A therapist who is truly neutral and child-focused will sound like this:

### On the Child's Emotional State
✅ "[Child] has been expressing sadness about transitions. We're working on identifying and naming those emotions."

✅ "[Child] drew a picture of both homes today and was able to identify positive things about each. That's real progress."

✅ "I've noticed some regression in [child's] behavior after long gaps between seeing you. This is clinically significant and I'd like to discuss it."

### On Treatment Approach
✅ "I use Trauma-Focused CBT, which is evidence-based for children experiencing high-conflict family situations. Here's a handout explaining the approach."

✅ "I'd like to incorporate both parents into the treatment. Research consistently shows that children do best when both parents are engaged in therapy."

### On Conflict Between Parents
✅ "My role is to support [child], not to take sides in your custody dispute. I won't be making custody recommendations—that's for the court."

✅ "I need both parents to agree on treatment goals. Can we schedule a meeting to align on this?"

### On Boundaries
✅ "I can share general progress and treatment information with both parents. I cannot share the specific content of what [child] says in session, as that therapeutic relationship requires trust. But I can share themes and clinical observations."

> 🧠 **Mental Model:** A competent therapist distinguishes between **session content** (what the child says in confidence) and **treatment information** (diagnosis, goals, progress, recommendations). Both parents are entitled to treatment information. Session content confidentiality protects the child, not one parent.

## Red Flags: When the Therapist Is Compromised {#red-flags}

These are the warning signs that the therapist has become an arm of the alienation campaign:

### 🚩 Red Flag #1: One-Sided Communication
The therapist communicates freely with one parent but is evasive, delayed, or unresponsive with the other.

**Evidence Strength: HIGH** — Document every unanswered email, unreturned call, and delayed response. Compare response times between parents.

### 🚩 Red Flag #2: Refusing to Meet With Both Parents
"I've decided it's best if I only communicate with [other parent]" or "The child is more comfortable if you're not involved."

**Evidence Strength: HIGH** — This violates APA Guideline 12 and NASW Standard 1.01. No ethical therapist excludes a custodial parent without a court order.

### 🚩 Red Flag #3: Validating the Child's Rejection Without Investigation
"[Child] says she doesn't want to see you, and I think we need to respect that."

A competent therapist would explore *why* the child feels this way, assess for coaching or enmeshment, and work toward repair—not validate rejection.

**Evidence Strength: CRITICAL** — This is the hallmark of a therapist who has been co-opted into the alienation dynamic.

### 🚩 Red Flag #4: Making Custody Recommendations
"I think [child] would be better off primarily with [other parent]" or writing letters supporting one parent's custody position.

Therapists are NOT custody evaluators. Making custody recommendations without a formal evaluation is an ethical violation and potentially grounds for a licensing complaint.

**Evidence Strength: HIGH** — Request any letters or reports the therapist has produced and review them for bias.

### 🚩 Red Flag #5: Only Scheduling During One Parent's Time
All sessions occur during Parent A's custody time. Parent B never has the opportunity to bring the child or participate.

**Evidence Strength: MEDIUM** — Could be logistical, but persistent patterns suggest alignment.

### 🚩 Red Flag #6: Using the Child's Statements Against You
"[Child] told me you yelled at her" or "[Child] says your house is scary" without clinical context, without exploring the statement, and delivered as accusation.

**Evidence Strength: HIGH** — A competent therapist reports clinical observations, not unverified child statements as fact.

### 🚩 Red Flag #7: Refusing to Provide Written Records
"I don't do summaries" or "My notes are for my eyes only."

You have a legal right to treatment records under [HIPAA](https://www.cdc.gov/). Refusal is a violation.

**Evidence Strength: HIGH** — File a formal HIPAA complaint if records are withheld.

### 🚩 Red Flag #8: Coordinating With the Other Parent's Attorney
The therapist is communicating with or providing information to the other parent's lawyer without your consent or a court order.

**Evidence Strength: CRITICAL** — This destroys any claim of neutrality and may constitute a breach of confidentiality.

## The 'I Can't Tell You That' Problem {#cant-tell-you}

The most common stonewalling tactic is the blanket claim of "confidentiality." Here's what's actually confidential and what isn't:

### What IS Confidential
- The specific words the child says in session
- Detailed session notes (in some states)
- The child's private disclosures that don't involve safety concerns

### What IS NOT Confidential From a Custodial Parent
- Diagnosis
- Treatment plan and goals
- General progress and clinical observations
- Recommendations for additional services
- Crisis situations (self-harm, abuse reports)
- Medication recommendations
- Behavioral patterns observed in session

> ⚠️ **Common Mistake:** Accepting "It's confidential" as a complete answer. Respond with: *"I understand session content may be confidential, but I am requesting treatment information as a parent with legal custody under HIPAA 45 CFR § 164.502(g). Please provide the diagnosis, treatment plan, and progress summary in writing within 30 days."*

## HIPAA and Custody: What Most Parents Get Wrong {#hipaa-custody}

HIPAA designates parents as **personal representatives** of minor children, [with full access to treatment records](https://www.childwelfare.gov/). The only exceptions:

1. **A court order specifically restricts access** (not just a custody order—a specific restriction on medical records)
2. **The minor has consented to treatment independently** (varies by state; typically ages 12-16 depending on jurisdiction)
3. **The provider reasonably believes the parent may be abusing the child** (requires documentation, not just one parent's accusation)

### Key HIPAA Provisions

- **45 CFR § 164.502(g):** Parents are personal representatives with access rights
- **45 CFR § 164.524:** Right to access and obtain copies of health records
- **45 CFR § 164.530(d):** Providers must document and comply with access requests within 30 days

> 💡 **Pro Insight:** If a therapist refuses access, file a complaint with the HHS Office for Civil Rights (OCR). This is free, can be done online, and creates a federal record of the therapist's non-compliance. OCR complaints carry real consequences for providers.

## How Alienating Parents Weaponize Therapy {#weaponize-therapy}

The alienating parent uses the therapist as a force multiplier through these documented tactics:

### Tactic 1: Handpicking the Therapist
Selecting a therapist without the other parent's input, then presenting it as a fait accompli: "[Child] has been seeing Dr. Smith for three months and it would be disruptive to change now."

### Tactic 2: Pre-Briefing the Therapist
Meeting with the therapist privately before treatment begins to establish a narrative: "The child is traumatized by the other parent" or "The other parent has anger issues."

### Tactic 3: Controlling the Narrative Through Session Scheduling
Always being the one to bring the child, debrief the child before and after sessions, and control what information the therapist receives.

### Tactic 4: Using the Therapist as a Witness
Asking the therapist to write letters, provide testimony, or make statements supporting their custody position.

### Tactic 5: Isolation Through 'Confidentiality'
Instructing the therapist (or coaching the child to tell the therapist) that the other parent should not be involved "for the child's safety."

> 🧠 **Mental Model:** The alienating parent doesn't just want the child to reject you. They want every professional in the child's life to validate that rejection. The therapist is the most dangerous ally because their opinion carries clinical authority.

## What to Do When the Therapist Stonewalls You {#therapist-stonewalls}

### Step 1: Put Everything in Writing
Send a formal letter (email with read receipt) requesting specific information:
- Diagnosis
- Treatment plan
- Progress summary
- Session dates and duration
- Any recommendations

Cite HIPAA 45 CFR § 164.502(g) and your state's family code.

### Step 2: Set a Deadline
"Please provide the requested information within 30 days as required by HIPAA."

### Step 3: Copy Your Attorney
CC your family law attorney on all correspondence. This signals that you're documenting and may take legal action.

### Step 4: File a HIPAA Complaint
If records are withheld, file with HHS Office for Civil Rights: [hhs.gov/hipaa/filing-a-complaint](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/filing-a-complaint/index.html)

### Step 5: File a Licensing Board Complaint
Contact your state's licensing board for the therapist's credential type (psychology board, social work board, counseling board).

### Step 6: Request a Court Order
Ask your attorney to file a motion requiring the therapist to provide records and/or attend a deposition.

### Step 7: Request a New Therapist
File a motion to replace the therapist with a mutually agreed-upon provider who will communicate equally with both parents.

## Questions to Ask in the First Session {#first-session}

Before therapy begins—or at your first parent meeting—ask these questions and **document the answers**:

1. **"How will you communicate with both parents?"** — The answer should include equal access, regular updates, and willingness to meet with each parent.

2. **"What is your experience with high-conflict custody cases?"** — Look for specific training, not just general experience.

3. **"How do you handle it when a child expresses a preference for one parent?"** — The answer should include exploration of the reasons, assessment for coaching, and a goal of healthy relationships with both parents.

4. **"Will you make custody recommendations?"** — The answer should be no. If they say yes, find a different therapist.

5. **"How will you handle requests for letters or reports for court?"** — Ethical answer: "I can provide treatment summaries but will not advocate for either parent's custody position."

6. **"What is your policy on sharing treatment information with each parent?"** — The answer should confirm equal access under HIPAA.

7. **"Have you received any training on parental alienation or interference with parent-child relationships?"** — This tells you whether they'll recognize the dynamic.

8. **"How will sessions be scheduled to include both parents' custody time?"** — The answer should demonstrate flexibility and fairness.

> 💡 **Pro Insight:** Record these answers (if legal in your state) or take detailed notes. If the therapist later violates their own stated policies, this documentation becomes powerful evidence of bias.

## When to File a Licensing Board Complaint {#licensing-complaint}

File a complaint when you can document:

- **Refusal to provide legally mandated information** to a custodial parent
- **Making custody recommendations** outside the scope of treatment
- **Dual relationships** (treating the child while advocating for one parent)
- **Failure to maintain neutrality** as demonstrated by pattern evidence
- **Coordination with one parent's attorney** without consent or court order
- **Breach of confidentiality** by sharing the child's disclosures selectively

### How to File

1. Identify the therapist's license type and governing board
2. Download the complaint form from the state board website
3. Attach all documentation (communication logs, timeline, evidence of bias)
4. File the complaint and retain confirmation
5. Inform your attorney so it can be referenced in court proceedings

> ⚠️ **Common Mistake:** Filing a complaint based on emotion rather than documentation. The board will dismiss vague complaints. You need specific dates, specific violations, and specific evidence. This is exactly what AntiAlienate helps you build.

## Frequently Asked Questions {#faqs}

### Can the therapist refuse to talk to me if my child says they don't want me involved?
No. A child's preference does not override a parent's legal right to treatment information. The therapist must distinguish between session content confidentiality and parental access to treatment data.

### What if the other parent selected the therapist without my consent?
You can object in writing and request the court appoint a mutually agreed-upon therapist. Document that you were excluded from the selection process.

### Can I attend my child's therapy sessions?
This depends on clinical judgment and court orders. However, you can request separate parent sessions and have a right to treatment information regardless of session attendance.

### What if the therapist says the child needs to 'feel safe' and that means excluding me?
"Feeling safe" is not a clinical standard that justifies excluding a custodial parent. Request the specific clinical basis for this recommendation in writing, with citations to evidence-based practice.

### Can I record conversations with the therapist?
Check your state's recording consent laws (one-party vs. two-party). Regardless, always take detailed written notes immediately after every interaction.

### What if the court ordered the therapy?
Court-ordered therapy typically specifies parameters. Review the order carefully—both parents usually have equal rights to information about court-ordered treatment.

## Final Takeaway {#final-takeaway}

Your child's therapist is not above the law. They are not above ethical standards. And they are not entitled to become a gatekeeper between you and your child's mental health.

**The standard is clear:**
- Both parents get the same information
- Both parents participate in treatment decisions
- The therapist remains neutral
- Treatment focuses on the child's relationship with **both** parents

Anything less than this standard is a red flag. Document it. Challenge it. Escalate it.

The parent who shows up with organized evidence of a therapist's bias—communication logs, timelines, ethical violation checklists—doesn't just protect their case. They protect their child from a professional who has forgotten their oath.

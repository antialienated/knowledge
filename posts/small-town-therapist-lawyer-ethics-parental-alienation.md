## The Small-Town Problem Nobody Talks About

In a city of 500,000, a therapist and a family lawyer may never cross paths. In a town of 15,000? They're on the same school board. Their kids play on the same team. They refer clients to each other.

This creates what ethics researchers call **"dual relationships"** — situations where a professional has more than one role or connection with a client or related party.

In parental alienation cases, dual relationships don't just create bias. They create a **closed system** where:

- The therapist validates the alienating parent's narrative
- The lawyer uses the therapist's "professional opinion" as evidence
- The targeted parent faces a united front disguised as independent professional judgment
- The court receives two "independent" opinions that are actually one coordinated strategy

💡 **Pro Insight**: In small jurisdictions, the referral network IS the power structure. A therapist who regularly receives referrals from a specific lawyer has a financial incentive to produce opinions favorable to that lawyer's clients — whether they realize it or not.

## Why Therapist-Lawyer Relationships Are Ethically Dangerous

### The Referral Pipeline Problem

When a lawyer consistently refers clients to the same therapist, an unspoken agreement forms:

- **The lawyer gets**: A "trusted" expert who produces predictable opinions
- **The therapist gets**: A steady stream of paying clients
- **The child gets**: A therapist whose objectivity is compromised before the first session

This isn't speculation. The American Psychological Association's (APA) Ethical Principles of Psychologists (Standard 3.05) explicitly warns against multiple relationships that could "reasonably be expected to impair the psychologist's objectivity."

### Information Contamination

In a proper clinical assessment, a therapist forms opinions based on direct observation and evidence. But when a therapist has a pre-existing relationship with one party's lawyer:

- They receive **pre-framed narratives** before meeting the family
- They enter the therapeutic relationship with **confirmation bias**
- They may unconsciously (or consciously) **filter observations** through the lens that serves their referral partner

⚠️ **Common Mistake**: Many targeted parents assume that because a therapist has credentials, their assessment must be objective. Credentials don't eliminate conflicts of interest — they just make them harder to challenge.

## How These Relationships Weaponize Alienation

### Pattern 1: The "Independent" Assessment That Isn't

The alienating parent's lawyer recommends a specific therapist for the child. The therapist conducts an "independent" assessment and — surprise — concludes that the child's resistance to the targeted parent is organic, not coached.

What the court doesn't know: The therapist and lawyer have referred dozens of clients to each other over 10 years.

### Pattern 2: The Therapist as Gatekeeper

The therapist becomes the person who decides:

- Whether the targeted parent can attend sessions
- What information is shared with the court
- Whether reunification is "recommended"
- When the child is "ready" for contact

Every one of these decisions happens to align with the alienating parent's wishes. Every one.

### Pattern 3: The Coordinated Narrative

The lawyer files a motion citing the therapist's "professional recommendation." The therapist writes a letter supporting the lawyer's motion. Each cites the other as independent validation.

This is not collaboration. This is **collusion disguised as clinical judgment.**

### Pattern 4: The Information Silo

The therapist shares detailed session notes with the alienating parent's lawyer but:

- Claims "confidentiality" when the targeted parent requests records
- Provides only sanitized summaries to the court
- Fails to document the child's statements that contradict the alienation narrative

🧠 **Mental Model**: Think of it like insider trading. When a therapist and a lawyer share privileged information outside proper channels, they're trading on inside knowledge — except the currency isn't money. It's your child.

## The Ethical Standards That Are Being Violated

### APA Ethics Code Violations

| Standard | Requirement | How It's Violated |
|---|---|---|
| 3.05 Multiple Relationships | Avoid relationships that impair objectivity | Therapist has financial/social ties to one party's lawyer |
| 3.06 Conflict of Interest | Refrain when personal interests could impair work | Referral income creates financial conflict |
| 9.01 Bases for Assessments | Base opinions on sufficient examination | Pre-framed narratives replace independent assessment |
| 2.04 Bases for Scientific/Professional Judgment | Ground work in established knowledge | Ignoring alienation research to maintain referral relationship |

### AFCC Guidelines

The Association of Family and Conciliation Courts (AFCC) Model Standards for Child Custody Evaluation (2006) specifically address:

- **Standard 4.4**: Evaluators should be alert to the ways in which their own values, assumptions, and relationship history may affect their work
- **Standard 4.7**: Evaluators should decline appointments when they have personal or professional relationships with parties

### Belgian-Specific: Codex Deontologie

For practitioners in Belgium:

- **Article 28**: Psychologists must maintain independence and impartiality
- **Article 29**: Professional relationships must not be influenced by financial interests or personal relationships
- **Article 33**: When a conflict of interest exists or is likely, the psychologist must decline or withdraw

The **Psychologencommissie** (Belgian Psychology Commission) has disciplinary authority and can revoke authorization to practice.

## Red Flags: Signs Your Therapist Has a Conflict of Interest

Watch for these warning signs:

1. **The therapist was recommended by the opposing lawyer** — not the court, not your attorney, not an independent source
2. **The therapist's office is in the same building** or block as the opposing lawyer
3. **The therapist seems to know details about your case** before you've shared them
4. **The therapist resists your involvement** in your child's treatment from the start
5. **Reports and letters align perfectly** with the opposing lawyer's legal arguments
6. **The therapist refuses to provide records** to you while seemingly sharing information with the other side
7. **The therapist attends social events** with the opposing lawyer (check social media, community events)
8. **Multiple families report** the same therapist-lawyer pairing producing the same outcomes
9. **The therapist dismisses alienation** as a concept without engagement with the research
10. **Recommendations always favor** one party — never balanced, never nuanced

💡 **Pro Insight**: Check the therapist's Google reviews. In small towns, other targeted parents often leave coded warnings. Phrases like "seemed to have their mind made up" or "only listened to one side" are red flags.

## How to Document and Expose the Conflict

### Step 1: Map the Relationship

- Search for **joint presentations**, conference panels, or co-authored papers between the therapist and lawyer
- Check **LinkedIn connections** and mutual endorsements
- Look for **shared office addresses** or practice affiliations
- Request from the court: **How many cases has this therapist been appointed or recommended in by this lawyer?**
- FOIA/transparency requests for any **government contracts** involving both parties

### Step 2: Document the Pattern

Create a timeline showing:

- When the therapist was recommended and by whom
- Every instance where the therapist's recommendations aligned with the lawyer's motions
- Specific clinical conclusions that contradict established research on alienation
- Any information that appears to have been shared between therapist and lawyer outside proper channels

### Step 3: Request Records Formally

Under HIPAA (US) or GDPR (EU/Belgium), you have the right to:

- Complete session notes
- All communications regarding your child
- The therapist's correspondence with any attorneys
- Referral records showing who sent clients to whom

⚠️ **Common Mistake**: Don't confront the therapist verbally about the conflict. This triggers the 'difficult parent' counter-narrative. Instead, put everything in writing. Written requests create evidence. Verbal confrontations create ammunition against you.

### Step 4: Get an Independent Second Opinion

Retain a therapist or evaluator from **outside the local jurisdiction** — ideally from a different city or region entirely. Their independent assessment serves as a control comparison.

## Filing Complaints: Step-by-Step

### In the United States

1. **State Licensing Board**: File a complaint with the therapist's state licensing board citing specific ethical violations
2. **APA Ethics Committee**: If the therapist is APA-member, file with the APA
3. **AFCC**: If the therapist serves as a court-appointed evaluator, report to AFCC
4. **Court Motion**: File a motion to disqualify the therapist, attaching evidence of the dual relationship

### In Belgium

1. **Psychologencommissie**: File a formal complaint (klacht/plainte) documenting the conflict of interest
2. **Orde van Artsen** (if the therapist is a psychiatrist): File with the medical disciplinary body
3. **Stafhouder/Bâtonnier**: If the lawyer participated in the ethical violation, file a separate complaint against the lawyer
4. **Court Motion**: Request the court appoint a truly independent expert from outside the judicial arrondissement

### What to Include in Your Complaint

- Specific ethical standards violated (cite by number)
- Timeline of events with dates
- Evidence of the therapist-lawyer relationship
- Examples of biased clinical conclusions
- Impact on the child and your parental rights
- Any records requests that were denied or delayed

## The Belgian Context: Codex Deontologie and Beyond

Belgium's small judicial arrondissements make this problem particularly acute. In cities like Mechelen, Dendermonde, or Tongeren, the entire family court ecosystem — judges, lawyers, therapists, court-appointed experts — may consist of fewer than 30 professionals.

### The Belgian Referral Loop

In Belgium, judges often ask lawyers to "suggest" an expert. The lawyer suggests the therapist they always work with. The judge, who also knows this therapist, approves the appointment. Nobody sees a conflict because **the conflict IS the system.**

### Criminal Liability

If a therapist in Belgium produces a report that contains demonstrably false statements to benefit a party they have a financial relationship with, this potentially constitutes:

- **Valsheid in geschrifte** (forgery in documents) — Belgian Criminal Code Arts. 193-214
- **Meineed** (perjury) if the therapist testifies under oath based on biased conclusions
- **Schriftvervalsing** when clinical records are altered or selectively presented

### Art. 458bis: The Duty to Report

Under Belgian law, professionals who become aware that a child is being harmed have a duty to report under Art. 458bis. A therapist who sees alienation occurring but fails to report it because doing so would harm their referral partner's client is violating this duty.

## What Courts Are Starting to Recognize

### The Tide Is Turning

Increasingly, courts are recognizing the danger of therapist-lawyer collaboration in custody cases:

- **ECHR (European Court of Human Rights)**: Article 8 rulings have emphasized the need for truly independent expert assessments in family matters
- **Belgian Hof van Cassatie**: Has overturned custody decisions where expert independence was not adequately verified
- **US Federal Courts**: The Daubert standard is increasingly applied to custody evaluators, requiring demonstration of methodological independence

### Judicial Best Practices Emerging

Some jurisdictions are now implementing:

- **Blind assignment** of court-appointed therapists (no party recommendations)
- **Conflict of interest disclosure forms** for all court-appointed experts
- **Rotation systems** that prevent the same therapist-lawyer pairs from repeatedly appearing together
- **Mandatory disclosure** of referral relationships in expert reports

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is it illegal for a therapist to accept referrals from a lawyer?

Not inherently illegal, but it creates an ethical obligation to disclose the relationship and recuse when objectivity could be compromised. When they fail to disclose, it becomes an ethics violation. When biased opinions result, it may become actionable fraud.

### What if the therapist is the only one in town?

This is the most common justification — and the weakest. The APA Ethics Code addresses this in Standard 3.05(c): even when avoidance isn't feasible, the psychologist must take steps to minimize harm. At minimum, full disclosure is required. Better: request a therapist from the nearest larger city.

### Can I sue the therapist for damages?

Yes, in most jurisdictions. If you can demonstrate that a therapist's biased assessment — influenced by their relationship with opposing counsel — resulted in loss of custody or parenting time, you may have a civil claim for professional malpractice and negligent infliction of emotional distress.

### How do I prove the therapist and lawyer have a relationship?

Public records, social media, joint event attendance, cross-referral patterns (request data from the court on how many times this pair has appeared together), shared office addresses, and testimony from other families who experienced the same pattern.

### What if the judge appointed this therapist?

A judge's appointment doesn't immunize a therapist from ethical obligations. File a motion challenging the appointment on conflict-of-interest grounds, supported by evidence of the dual relationship. Courts can and do replace appointed experts.

### Does this only happen in small towns?

No, but small towns amplify the problem exponentially. In larger cities, the same dynamics exist within specific practice groups, bar associations, or professional networks — but there are more alternatives available.

## Final Takeaway

The question isn't whether therapist-lawyer relationships in small towns are common. They are. The question is whether those relationships are being managed ethically — and in parental alienation cases, the evidence overwhelmingly suggests they are not.

When a therapist's paycheck depends on a lawyer's referrals, their "clinical objectivity" becomes a product they're selling — not a standard they're upholding.

**Your action steps:**

1. **Map the relationship** between your child's therapist and the opposing lawyer
2. **Document everything** in writing — never verbally
3. **Request records** under HIPAA/GDPR before they can be altered
4. **Get an independent assessment** from outside the local jurisdiction
5. **File formal complaints** with the appropriate licensing boards
6. **Move the court** to appoint a truly independent expert

Your child's wellbeing shouldn't depend on who golfs with whom on Sunday morning.

A 9-year-old says, *"I don't want to go to Dad's house."*

## What Is a Refusal Protocol? {#what-is-it}

A refusal protocol is a **pre-agreed set of steps** that adults follow when a child refuses contact with a parent. It removes improvisation and ensures that:

- The child's feelings are acknowledged
- The contact still occurs (or is rescheduled within a defined window)
- The aligned parent is held accountable for facilitating
- There are consequences for non-compliance

Think of it like a fire drill. You don't decide what to do *during* the fire. You follow the protocol.

## Why 'Respecting the Child's Wishes' Fails {#why-it-fails}

The phrase sounds humane. But consider:

- We don't let children decide whether to attend school
- We don't let children decide whether to go to the dentist
- We don't let children decide whether to wear a seatbelt

Yet somehow, in custody cases, a child's *stated wish* to avoid a parent is treated as sacrosanct.

**The paradox**: The same legal system that says children can't consent to contracts, vote, or drive is asking them to make binding decisions about their most important relationships.

💡 **Pro Insight**: Dr. Joan Kelly (2007) found that children's expressed preferences in high-conflict cases aligned with the preferences of whichever parent they were with at the time — not their actual feelings. The 'wish' was a loyalty performance, not an autonomous choice.

## The Avoidance Feedback Loop {#avoidance-loop}

Avoidance operates on a predictable neurological cycle:

1. **Child anticipates transition** → anxiety increases
2. **Child refuses** → aligned parent allows refusal
3. **Anxiety decreases immediately** → brain registers avoidance as 'successful'
4. **Next transition** → anxiety is *higher* because avoidance was reinforced
5. **Repeat** → each cycle makes contact harder, not easier

This is identical to how phobias develop. The treatment for phobias is *exposure*, not avoidance. Yet the family court system prescribes avoidance as treatment.

⚠️ **Common Mistake**: Interpreting the child's relief after avoiding contact as evidence that avoidance was the right call. The relief is real — and it's exactly what makes the cycle worse.

## Building an Effective Refusal Protocol {#building-protocol}

A good refusal protocol has five components:

### 1. Acknowledgment Script
*"I can see you're feeling upset about going to [parent's] house. It's okay to feel that way. We're still going."*

### 2. De-escalation Window
A defined period (15-30 minutes) for the child to calm down. NOT an indefinite delay.

### 3. Transition Support
The aligned parent physically facilitates the transition. Not 'I'll leave it up to them.' Not 'I told them to get in the car.'

### 4. Backup Plan
If transition fails: contact is rescheduled within 48 hours, and the aligned parent provides written explanation.

### 5. Documentation & Consequences
Every refusal is documented. Patterns trigger review. Repeated facilitation failures have escalating consequences (makeup time, modified orders, contempt).

## The Role of Each Adult {#adult-roles}

### The Aligned Parent Must:
- Speak positively about the other parent before transitions
- Physically prepare the child (packed bag, on time)
- Not ask the child 'Do you want to go?' (this creates a false choice)
- Follow the acknowledgment script
- Report to the parenting coordinator if transition fails

### The Rejected Parent Must:
- Remain calm and warm during transitions
- Not interrogate the child about the other household
- Not bribe or over-compensate
- Follow the structured activity plan for the visit
- Document everything

### The Professionals Must:
- Set the protocol in writing before a crisis occurs
- Not side with the child's stated wishes without evaluation
- Hold both parents accountable
- Report non-compliance to the court

🧠 **Mental Model**: A refusal protocol is like a custody order's immune system. Without it, any virus (a bad day, a coached statement, a tantrum) can kill the entire arrangement.

## When Refusal Protocols Are Inappropriate {#when-inappropriate}

Refusal protocols should NOT be used when:

- There are **substantiated** (not alleged) safety concerns
- The child shows clinical signs of genuine trauma from the parent
- A forensic evaluation has confirmed realistic estrangement
- The rejected parent has untreated substance abuse or domestic violence

The key word is **substantiated.** Allegations without investigation are not a basis for suspending contact.

## Frequently Asked Questions {#faqs}

### What age should refusal protocols apply to?
All ages. The script changes, the approach softens for teens, but the principle (adults make custody decisions, not children) applies universally.

### What if the child becomes physically resistant?
The protocol should have a specific physical resistance clause. Typically: the transition is paused (not canceled), a professional is called, and the visit is rescheduled within 24-48 hours.

### Can a judge order a refusal protocol?
Yes. Many jurisdictions allow courts to include detailed transition protocols in custody orders. Request it specifically.

### What if the aligned parent claims the child is 'sick' every transition?
Document the pattern. Three or more last-minute 'illnesses' should trigger a court review and potential contempt proceedings.

## Final Takeaway {#final-takeaway}

When adults fail to create refusal protocols, they hand custody decisions to the person least equipped to make them: the child.

A child's refusal isn't a verdict. It's a signal. And the adults around that child have a responsibility to respond with structure, not surrender.

**Avoidance is not a parenting plan. Structure is.**

# Should You Confront a Therapist Before Filing an Ethics Complaint?

{#introduction}

*Three different professionals, three different strategic calculations — and the critical mistakes parents make with each one.*

---

In high-conflict custody, the word "therapist" gets thrown around as if it's one role. It's not. **Your child's individual therapist, the family therapist, and your own personal therapist** each operate under different ethical obligations, different relationship dynamics, and different strategic risks.

The question of whether to confront them before filing an ethics complaint has a **completely different answer** depending on which therapist you're dealing with.

This guide breaks down the tactical considerations for each — because the wrong move with the wrong therapist can cost you your case.

> ⚠️ **Common Mistake:** Treating all therapists in your custody case as interchangeable. They are not. Each has a distinct relationship to you, your child, and the court — and each requires a different strategic approach.

## Understanding the Three Therapist Roles

{#three-roles}

Before making any move, you must understand the fundamentally different positions these three professionals occupy:

| Role | Primary Client | Your Relationship | Court Influence | Risk Level |
|------|---------------|-------------------|----------------|------------|
| **Child's Therapist** | Your child | Third party (you are not the patient) | Very High — often consulted by GAL/evaluator | 🔴 Critical |
| **Family Therapist** | The family unit / co-parenting dynamic | Joint participant | High — recommendations carry weight | 🟠 High |
| **Your Therapist** | You | Direct therapeutic relationship | Medium — your records may be subpoenaed | 🟡 Moderate |

> 🧠 **Mental Model:** Think of it as three concentric circles of risk. The further the therapist is from your direct control, the more dangerous a confrontation becomes.

## Part 1: Your Child's Individual Therapist

{#childs-therapist}

### Why This Is the Highest-Risk Confrontation

Your child's therapist is the **most dangerous** professional to confront prematurely. Here's why:

**They are not your therapist.** You have limited standing in the therapeutic relationship. Your access to information is governed by HIPAA, state family codes, and often by court orders. The therapist's primary allegiance is (or should be) to the child — but in alienation cases, that allegiance is frequently co-opted by the alienating parent.

### Common Violations by a Child's Therapist

- **Refusing to communicate with both parents** (APA Standard 10.02 — Therapy Involving Couples/Families; state family codes requiring equal access)
- **Making custody recommendations without a proper evaluation** (APA 9.01 — Bases for Assessment)
- **Only receiving information from one parent** and forming treatment goals based on that skewed input
- **Failing to report signs of alienation** or coaching when the child presents clear indicators
- **Sharing session content with one parent but not the other**

### Should You Confront the Child's Therapist?

**In most cases: NO.**

The reasons are critical:

1. **Record alteration risk is highest here.** The child's therapist controls session notes that directly influence custody evaluators, GALs, and judges. A confrontation gives them time to retroactively "clarify" their notes.

2. **The "hostile parent" trap.** Any contact from you — even calm, professional contact — can be documented as "parent attempted to influence child's treatment" or "parent exhibited controlling behavior regarding therapeutic process."

3. **Alignment with the alienating parent.** In alienation cases, the child's therapist has often already been recruited into the alienating parent's narrative. Your confrontation won't break that alliance; it will reinforce it.

4. **The child's therapist has the most direct pipeline to the court.** Their characterization of you will echo through evaluations, GAL reports, and judicial decisions.

### The Strategic Move

1. **File a HIPAA records request** for your child's complete treatment records — treatment plans, session notes, communications logs
2. **Document the specific violations** with dates, ethical standards, and supporting evidence
3. **Consult your attorney** about timing relative to your custody proceedings
4. **File the ethics complaint without warning** to preserve the integrity of existing records
5. **Simultaneously consider a Motion to Disqualify** the therapist from providing testimony or recommendations

> 💡 **Pro Insight:** If the child's therapist has been seeing your child for months and has never contacted you, never included you in treatment planning, and only communicates with the other parent — that is not an oversight. That is a pattern. Document it as such.

### One Exception

If the issue is purely administrative (e.g., the therapist hasn't responded to a scheduling request), a single written inquiry is appropriate. But frame it as a records request, not a complaint:

> *"I am requesting clarification on [Child's Name]'s current appointment schedule and confirmation that both parents are being notified of sessions per [court order / state family code]."*

## Part 2: The Family Therapist / Reunification Therapist

{#family-therapist}

### The Unique Dynamics

A family therapist or reunification therapist occupies a strange middle ground: you are a **participant** in the therapy, but you are not the sole client. The "client" is the family unit or the co-parenting relationship.

This creates a specific ethical tension: the therapist must maintain neutrality between parties, yet in alienation cases, one party is actively undermining the process.

### Common Violations by a Family Therapist

- **Taking sides** — forming an alliance with the alienating parent while pathologizing the targeted parent
- **Failing to address alienation dynamics** when they are clearly present in session
- **Sharing session content with one parent outside of sessions** (phone calls, side meetings)
- **Making recommendations to the court** that exceed their role (e.g., recommending supervised visitation when they were appointed for reunification)
- **Refusing to document one parent's participation** accurately
- **Conducting "reunification" therapy that is actually compliance training** — forcing the child to accept the alienating parent's narrative

### Should You Confront the Family Therapist?

**It depends — but lean toward caution.**

Because you are an active participant in family therapy, you have more standing than with your child's therapist. However, the risks remain significant:

**Arguments for confronting (carefully):**
- You have direct observation of the bias in sessions
- Raising concerns *in writing between sessions* documents your advocacy
- Courts expect participants in family therapy to "engage in the process" — staying silent can be used against you

**Arguments against confronting:**
- The family therapist may document your concerns as "resistance to the therapeutic process"
- If the therapist is already biased, confrontation triggers defensive alignment with the other party
- Your complaint in session can be reframed as "parent is not willing to take accountability"

### The Strategic Move

1. **Document every session** — Write detailed notes immediately after each session (date, time, who said what, therapist's responses, any concerning statements)
2. **Send a written "clarification request"** between sessions — not a complaint, but a neutral inquiry

> *"Following our session on [date], I want to ensure my understanding is accurate. I expressed concern about [specific issue]. Could you confirm how this will be addressed in our treatment plan? I want to make sure both parents' perspectives are being documented equally."*

3. **If the pattern continues (2-3 sessions),** escalate through your attorney
4. **File the ethics complaint after exiting therapy** if possible — filing while still in active therapy creates complications
5. **Your session notes become your evidence** — They are contemporaneous records that carry weight with ethics boards

> ⚠️ **Common Mistake:** Confronting the family therapist *in session* in front of the other parent. This hands the alienating parent a narrative: "See? They're the difficult one. They can't even cooperate in therapy."

### The Reunification Therapy Trap

If you are in court-ordered reunification therapy and the therapist is failing:

- **Do NOT stop attending.** Non-compliance with court orders is used against you.
- **Document the failures in writing between sessions.**
- **File a motion with the court** to appoint a different reunification therapist, citing specific failures with dates.
- **The ethics complaint and the motion can be filed simultaneously** — they operate on different tracks.

## Part 3: Your Own Personal Therapist

{#your-therapist}

### Why This Is Different

Your personal therapist is **your** therapist. You are the client. They owe their ethical obligations directly to you. This fundamentally changes the calculus.

### Common Violations by Your Own Therapist

- **Breaking confidentiality** — sharing your session content with the other parent, their attorney, or a custody evaluator without your written consent
- **Providing opinions to the court** about your fitness as a parent based on therapy sessions (which are treatment, not evaluation)
- **Dual relationships** — also treating the other parent, or having a relationship with someone involved in the case
- **Failing to maintain proper boundaries** when subpoenaed — not asserting privilege on your behalf
- **Testifying beyond their competence** — a therapist who treats your anxiety cannot opine on your parenting capacity

### Should You Confront Your Own Therapist?

**Yes — in most cases, this is the one therapist you SHOULD confront first.**

Here's why:

1. **You have a direct therapeutic relationship.** Raising concerns is actually part of healthy therapy. Ethics boards will note whether the client attempted to address concerns directly.

2. **Your therapist has less court influence.** Unlike the child's therapist or family therapist, your personal therapist's records are protected by privilege (in most jurisdictions) unless you waive it.

3. **The risk of record alteration is lower.** Your therapist's notes about you are less likely to be shared with custody evaluators unless subpoenaed — and you can assert privilege.

4. **Confrontation here demonstrates emotional maturity.** If the issue reaches the court, showing that you addressed it directly first strengthens your position.

### The Strategic Move

1. **Raise the concern in session** and follow up with a written summary:

> *"As we discussed in our session on [date], I have concerns about [specific issue — e.g., the release of my records to the opposing counsel without my written authorization]. I want to confirm that this has been addressed and that my confidentiality will be maintained going forward."*

2. **Give them a reasonable window (10-14 days) to respond and correct**
3. **If they correct the issue** — document it, continue therapy, and monitor
4. **If they do not correct** — file the ethics complaint with your documented communication as evidence that you attempted resolution
5. **Consider changing therapists** — but do so strategically, not emotionally

### The Subpoena Trap

The most common crisis with your own therapist in custody cases:

- The opposing side subpoenas your therapy records
- Your therapist either (a) releases them without asserting privilege on your behalf, or (b) provides testimony that goes beyond treatment observations

**If this happens:**
- This is an immediate ethics violation (breach of confidentiality)
- Confront in writing immediately and cc your attorney
- File a motion to quash the subpoena if records were released improperly
- File the ethics complaint — the breach has already occurred, and confronting first documents your awareness

## The Universal Paper Trail Protocol

{#paper-trail-protocol}

Regardless of which therapist you're dealing with, execute this sequence BEFORE any confrontation or complaint:

### Step 1: Gather Records
- **Child's therapist:** HIPAA request for child's complete records
- **Family therapist:** Request for session notes, treatment plan, and communications log
- **Your therapist:** Request for your complete file (you are entitled to this under HIPAA)

### Step 2: Document Violations

| Date | Therapist Type | Violation | Ethical Standard | Evidence |
|------|---------------|-----------|-----------------|----------|
| | Child's | Refused parent access | APA 10.02, State Family Code | Email denial |
| | Family | Side meeting with other parent | APA 3.05 (Multiple Relationships) | Calendar conflict + notes |
| | Yours | Released records without consent | APA 4.01 (Privacy), HIPAA | Opposing counsel filing |

### Step 3: Secure Communications
Screenshot and preserve every email, portal message, and text. Include metadata (timestamps, read receipts).

### Step 4: Consult Your Attorney
Your attorney must assess timing relative to custody proceedings for ALL THREE scenarios.

## What Ethics Boards Actually Evaluate

{#what-boards-evaluate}

Ethics complaints are evaluated on four criteria — none of which require prior confrontation:

1. **Was a specific ethical standard violated?** (APA, AAMFT, NASW, or state licensing board codes)
2. **Is there documentation supporting the claim?**
3. **Did the violation cause or risk harm?**
4. **Is there a pattern of conduct?**

> 💡 **Pro Insight:** APA Ethics Code Section 1.04 (Informal Resolution) suggests attempting resolution when feasible — but explicitly excepts situations involving litigation, custody disputes, or risk of evidence tampering. Your custody case IS the exception.

## Side-by-Side: Confront vs. File Decision Matrix

{#decision-matrix}

| Factor | Child's Therapist | Family Therapist | Your Therapist |
|--------|-------------------|------------------|----------------|
| **Confront first?** | ❌ Almost never | ⚠️ Carefully, in writing | ✅ Usually yes |
| **Record alteration risk** | 🔴 Very High | 🟠 High | 🟡 Low-Medium |
| **Court influence** | 🔴 Direct & High | 🟠 Significant | 🟡 Limited (privileged) |
| **"Hostile parent" risk** | 🔴 Extreme | 🟠 Moderate | 🟢 Low |
| **Privilege protection** | ❌ None (not your therapist) | ⚠️ Limited | ✅ Strong |
| **Ethics board expectation** | No confrontation required | Documented concern helpful | Informal resolution preferred |
| **Best timing for complaint** | Before custody evaluation | After exiting therapy if possible | After giving correction window |

## What Your Attorney Needs to Know

{#what-attorney-needs}

Before filing ANY therapist ethics complaint during custody litigation, your attorney must evaluate:

1. **Which therapist is the target** — The strategic calculus differs completely
2. **Timing relative to custody proceedings** — Filing during active evaluation may appear retaliatory
3. **Whether a Motion to Disqualify is more effective** — Often faster and more impactful than a board complaint
4. **Parallel legal remedies** — Some violations warrant both an ethics complaint AND a court motion
5. **Insurance and liability implications** — A complaint may trigger malpractice coverage, changing the therapist's behavior

> 💡 **Pro Insight:** A **Motion to Disqualify** against the child's therapist or family therapist is often more immediately impactful than an ethics complaint (which takes 6-18 months). Your attorney can file both simultaneously.

## Frequently Asked Questions

{#faqs}

**Q: Can I file ethics complaints against more than one therapist at the same time?**
A: Yes. Each complaint is evaluated independently by the relevant licensing board. However, coordinate timing with your attorney — filing against three therapists simultaneously may be perceived as scorched-earth litigation.

**Q: The child's therapist says I have no right to information. Is that true?**
A: In most jurisdictions, both custodial parents have equal access to their child's treatment information unless a court order specifically restricts it. Cite HIPAA 45 CFR § 164.524 and your state family code.

**Q: The family therapist told me I'm "not engaging in the process." What do I do?**
A: Document this characterization in your post-session notes. Send a written response: *"I want to confirm my commitment to the process. Could you clarify what specific actions would demonstrate engagement?"* Their response (or non-response) becomes evidence.

**Q: My own therapist was subpoenaed. Can I stop them from testifying?**
A: In most jurisdictions, therapist-patient privilege belongs to the patient (you). Your attorney can file a motion to quash the subpoena. If your therapist testified without your consent, that is a separate ethics violation.

**Q: Will confronting my child's therapist make me look reasonable to the judge?**
A: Rarely. Judges in high-conflict cases are aware that therapist confrontations are often weaponized. Your documented paper trail demonstrates more sophistication than a confrontation.

**Q: The family therapist is court-appointed. Does that change anything?**
A: Court appointment does not exempt a therapist from ethical standards. However, your complaint pathway may include filing a motion with the appointing judge in addition to the licensing board.

**Q: Can a therapist retaliate against me for filing a complaint?**
A: Retaliation is itself an ethics violation (APA Standard 1.07). Document any changes in the therapist's behavior after your filing — this strengthens your case.

**Q: Should I tell my child's therapist I'm considering a complaint?**
A: **Absolutely not.** This is the single most common mistake parents make. It gives the therapist time to align records, consult their own attorney, and potentially prejudice the evaluator against you.

## Final Takeaway

{#final-takeaway}

The answer to "Should I confront the therapist first?" depends entirely on **which therapist**:

- **Your child's therapist:** Almost never confront. Document, file records requests, and file the complaint without warning. The stakes are too high and the risk of record alteration is too great.

- **The family therapist:** Proceed with extreme caution. Written clarification requests between sessions are acceptable; in-session confrontations are dangerous. File after exiting therapy if possible.

- **Your own therapist:** This is the one case where confrontation is usually appropriate. You have a direct therapeutic relationship, privilege protections, and ethics boards do consider whether informal resolution was attempted.

**The universal rule:** No matter which therapist, the paper trail comes first. HIPAA requests, documentation logs, attorney consultation — then and only then do you make your move.

Your child needs a strategic parent, not an emotional one. Know which battles to fight silently and which to fight directly.

**Facts over feelings. Evidence that speaks.**

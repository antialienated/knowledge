## Why Police Reports Matter in Belgian Custody Cases {#why-police-reports-matter}

In Belgian family law, a police report (*procès-verbal* or *PV*) is one of the most powerful pieces of evidence you can present to a family court judge. Unlike personal notes or verbal testimony, a PV carries **official evidentiary weight** under Belgian procedural law.

Here's why this matters:

- **Article 1253ter/4-6 of the Belgian Judicial Code** governs urgent custody matters. A documented PV showing violations of custody arrangements can trigger emergency hearings.
- Belgian family court judges rely heavily on **objective documentation**. A police report creates a timestamped, official record that your co-parent cannot later deny or rewrite.
- The *Procureur des Konings / Procureur du Roi* (King's Prosecutor) reviews PVs to decide whether criminal prosecution is warranted under **Article 432 of the Belgian Penal Code** (non-representation of a child).

> 💡 **Pro Insight:** Belgian judges have explicitly stated in published jurisprudence that parents who systematically document violations via official channels are viewed as more credible than those who rely solely on text messages or verbal claims.

⚠️ **Common Mistake:** Many parents assume that sending a text message or email to their ex constitutes "proof." In Belgian courts, **only official documentation (PVs, bailiff reports, or notarized statements) carries full evidentiary weight.**

## Understanding the Belgian Police System {#belgian-police-system}

Belgium has a **two-tier police system** that you must understand before filing:

### Local Police (*Politiezone / Zone de Police*)

- **193 local police zones** across Belgium, each serving one or more municipalities
- Handle day-to-day complaints, neighborhood disputes, and initial custody violation reports
- Every zone has a **commissariat** (police station) where you can file a complaint
- Operating hours vary, but most have a **24/7 front desk** for urgent matters

### Federal Police (*Federale Politie / Police Fédérale*)

- Handle complex, cross-jurisdictional, or international cases
- Relevant if your custody dispute involves **cross-border abduction** (Hague Convention cases)
- Specialized units include the **Central Authority for International Child Abduction**

### Which One Do You Need?

| Situation | Go To |
|-----------|-------|
| Missed custody exchange | Local Police |
| Child not returned on time | Local Police |
| Verbal threats during exchange | Local Police |
| Physical violence or immediate danger | Local Police (or call 101/112) |
| Child taken across Belgian borders | Federal Police + Local Police |
| Systematic alienation pattern | Local Police (build file over time) |
| Violation of court order | Local Police |

> 🧠 **Mental Model:** Think of local police as your "first responders" for custody documentation. They create the official paper trail. Federal police step in when situations escalate beyond local jurisdiction.

## When to File a Police Report {#when-to-file}

Timing is everything. Belgian law and judicial practice create specific windows where filing is most effective.

### File IMMEDIATELY (Within Hours) When:

1. **Your child is not returned** at the court-ordered time — Under Article 432 of the Belgian Penal Code, *non-représentation d'enfant* is a criminal offense punishable by up to one year imprisonment
2. **Physical violence occurs** during or around a custody exchange
3. **Your child reports abuse** during the other parent's custody time
4. **The other parent flees with the child** to an unknown location
5. **You are denied court-ordered access** to your child (blocked from school pickup, denied entry, etc.)

### File Within 24-48 Hours When:

1. **Systematic schedule violations** (late returns, shortened visits)
2. **Parental alienation behaviors** witnessed during exchanges (child coached to refuse, disparaging remarks made in front of the child)
3. **Violations of specific court order provisions** (e.g., failing to provide passport, blocking medical appointments)
4. **Harassment via communication channels** (threatening messages, excessive calling during your parenting time)

### File Proactively (Building Your File) When:

1. **You notice a pattern developing** — Three or more incidents of the same type
2. **Before a court hearing** — File reports for any undocumented recent incidents
3. **When your attorney advises** creating an official record

> 💡 **Pro Insight:** Belgian family court judges look at **patterns, not isolated incidents.** Filing consistently over time creates a documentary trail that is far more persuasive than a single dramatic complaint. This is the "Slow Leak" principle — gradual, documented evidence of systematic behavior.

⚠️ **Common Mistake:** Waiting weeks or months to file a report. Belgian police and judges view delayed complaints with skepticism. The question becomes: "If it was serious enough to report, why did you wait?"

## Where to Go: Your Filing Options {#where-to-go}

### Option 1: Your Local Police Station (*Commissariat*)

**Best for:** Most custody-related complaints

**How to find yours:**
- Visit [www.police.be](https://www.police.be) and enter your postal code
- Or search "politiezone + [your municipality]" / "zone de police + [your commune]"
- Each zone lists its commissariats with addresses and opening hours

**Key tips:**
- Go during **weekday morning hours (8:00-12:00)** when staffing is highest and officers are freshest
- Avoid Friday afternoons and weekends unless urgent — reduced staffing means longer waits and potentially less thorough documentation
- Some zones allow **appointments** — call ahead to check

### Option 2: The Prosecutor's Office (*Parquet*)

You can file a complaint directly with the *Procureur du Roi / Procureur des Konings* (King's Prosecutor).

**Best for:**
- When police have been unresponsive to previous complaints
- When you want to ensure criminal follow-up
- Serious violations (child abduction, severe alienation with documented evidence)

**How:**
- Write a formal letter (*plainte*) addressed to the Prosecutor of the judicial district (*arrondissement judiciaire*) where you or the child resides
- Send via registered mail (*recommandé / aangetekend*) for proof of delivery
- Include all supporting documentation

### Option 3: Online Filing (*Police-on-Web*)

Belgium offers online police reporting at [www.police-on-web.be](https://www.police-on-web.be)

**Best for:** Non-urgent situations, property-related complaints

**NOT recommended for custody matters.** Online reports lack the personal interaction needed to ensure your complaint is properly categorized and detailed. Always file custody-related complaints **in person.**

### Option 4: The *Huissier de Justice / Gerechtsdeurwaarder* (Bailiff)

Not a police report, but a powerful complement:

- A bailiff can **witness and officially document** a failed custody exchange
- A bailiff's report (*constat*) has even higher evidentiary weight than a police PV in civil proceedings
- Cost: approximately €150-300 per intervention
- Can be arranged in advance for predictable exchanges

> 💡 **Pro Insight:** The most effective strategy is **both** — a police PV for the criminal record AND a bailiff's constat for the civil/family court record. This dual-documentation approach is what experienced Belgian family lawyers recommend for serious violations.

## What to Bring: Your Evidence Kit {#what-to-bring}

Arrive at the police station **prepared.** Officers are more likely to take your complaint seriously and document it thoroughly when you present organized evidence.

### Essential Documents (Always Bring):

- ✅ **Your identity card** (*carte d'identité / identiteitskaart*)
- ✅ **A copy of the court order** (*jugement / vonnis*) specifying custody arrangements — highlight the relevant provisions
- ✅ **A chronological written summary** of what happened — dates, times, locations, exact quotes where possible
- ✅ **Previous PV numbers** if you've filed before — this helps police link your complaints into a single dossier

### Supporting Evidence (Bring What You Have):

- 📱 **Screenshots of relevant messages** (WhatsApp, SMS, email) — printed, not just on your phone
- 📸 **Photos or videos** documenting the incident (e.g., empty exchange point, locked door, child's condition)
- 👤 **Witness contact information** — names, addresses, phone numbers of anyone who saw the incident
- 📅 **A calendar showing the custody schedule** with violations marked
- 📋 **A log of previous incidents** with dates and any PV numbers

### Professional Documentation (If Available):

- 🏥 Medical reports if a child shows signs of distress or injury
- 🎓 School attendance records showing absences during the other parent's time
- 👨‍⚕️ Therapist or psychologist notes (if shared with your consent)

> ⚠️ **Common Mistake:** Showing up unprepared and rambling emotionally. Police officers process dozens of complaints daily. **A parent who arrives with an organized folder and a clear, written timeline is taken far more seriously** than one who arrives upset and tells a disjointed story.

## Step-by-Step: How to File {#step-by-step}

### Step 1: Request to File a Formal Complaint (*Plainte / Klacht*)

When you arrive at the police station, clearly state:

🇫🇷 *"Je souhaite déposer une plainte formelle pour non-représentation d'enfant"* (I want to file a formal complaint for non-representation of a child)

🇳🇱 *"Ik wil een formele klacht indienen voor het niet-afgeven van een kind"*

**Critical distinction:** Insist on a *plainte* (formal complaint), NOT a *déclaration* or *main courante* (informal statement). Only a formal complaint triggers investigation obligations.

### Step 2: Provide Your Sworn Statement

The officer will take your statement (*audition / verhoor*). During this:

- **Stick to facts.** Dates, times, locations, exact words spoken.
- **Reference the court order** by its date and the court that issued it.
- **Mention Article 432 of the Penal Code** if access was denied — this signals that you understand the criminal nature of the violation.
- **Request the officer note all supporting evidence** you provide.

### Step 3: Ensure Proper Documentation

Before you leave, verify:

- ✅ You receive a **copy of the PV** or at minimum the **PV number** (*numéro de PV / PV-nummer*)
- ✅ The complaint is categorized correctly (e.g., *non-représentation d'enfant*, not "family dispute")
- ✅ All evidence you provided is listed in the PV
- ✅ You understand the next steps — will the PV be forwarded to the Prosecutor? When?

### Step 4: Get Your Copy

You have a legal right to receive a copy of your PV. Under the **Law of March 12, 1998** (*Wet Franchimont / Loi Franchimont*), victims have the right to:

- A copy of their complaint
- Information about the status of the investigation
- Be informed of any prosecution decisions

> 💡 **Pro Insight:** Some officers may try to discourage formal complaints for custody matters, suggesting you "work it out" or contact your lawyer instead. **You have an absolute legal right to file a formal complaint.** If an officer refuses, ask to speak with the *commissaire* (station commander) or note the officer's badge number and file a complaint with the *Comité P* (police oversight committee).

## The Do's: Best Practices That Judges Notice {#the-dos}

### ✅ DO: Be Calm, Factual, and Organized

Belgian police and judges respond to **composure and precision.** Even if you're shaking inside, present your case methodically.

### ✅ DO: File Every Single Time

Every denied visit, every late return, every violation. **Consistency is your superpower.** A judge reviewing 12 PVs over 6 months sees a pattern. A judge reviewing 1 PV sees an isolated incident.

### ✅ DO: Use the BIFF Method in Your Written Summary

- **Brief:** Keep your written statement concise
- **Informative:** Include only verifiable facts
- **Friendly:** Maintain a neutral, non-hostile tone
- **Firm:** Be clear about what happened and what you want documented

### ✅ DO: Reference Your Court Order by Date and Court

Example: *"Per the judgment of the Family Court of Brussels dated January 15, 2026, docket number 2025/1234/A, the child is to be returned to me every Sunday at 18:00."*

### ✅ DO: Request Police Escort for Future Exchanges

If exchanges have become hostile, you can request police presence at future custody exchanges. Belgian police zones can arrange *standby assistance* (*bijstand / assistance*).

### ✅ DO: Keep a Certified Copy of Every PV

Store copies in:
- A physical folder for your lawyer
- A digital backup (scan/photo)
- Your AntiAlienate case file for cross-referencing with other evidence

### ✅ DO: Bring a Witness When Possible

A family member, friend, or neutral third party who can corroborate your account adds credibility.

## The Don'ts: Mistakes That Destroy Your Case {#the-donts}

### ❌ DON'T: Exaggerate or Embellish

Belgian prosecutors and judges are trained to spot inconsistencies. **One exaggerated claim can discredit your entire file.** Stick to what happened, exactly as it happened.

### ❌ DON'T: File Frivolous or Retaliatory Complaints

Filing false or exaggerated police reports is a criminal offense under **Article 209 of the Belgian Penal Code** (*dénonciation calomnieuse / lasterlijke aangifte*). More practically, a judge who sees a pattern of minor, retaliatory complaints will view YOU as the problem parent.

### ❌ DON'T: Use the Police as a Weapon

The purpose of filing is to **document violations**, not to harass your co-parent. Belgian judges are highly attuned to weaponized police filings.

### ❌ DON'T: Argue with the Officer

If an officer seems dismissive:
- Calmly insist on your right to file
- Ask for a supervisor
- Note the officer's badge number
- Follow up with the Prosecutor's office directly

### ❌ DON'T: Bring Your Children to the Police Station

Unless absolutely necessary (e.g., they're reporting abuse directly), **do not bring your children to file complaints.** It can be used against you as evidence of involving children in adult conflicts.

### ❌ DON'T: Record the Police Interaction Without Permission

Recording police officers in Belgium without their consent can be problematic. Focus on getting everything documented in the official PV instead.

### ❌ DON'T: Post About It on Social Media

Anything you post online can and will be used against you. **Document privately, share legally.**

### ❌ DON'T: Accept an Informal Statement (*Main Courante*) as Sufficient

A *main courante* is a simple notation — it does NOT trigger investigation or prosecution. Always insist on a formal *plainte* with a PV number.

## After Filing: Critical Follow-Up Steps {#after-filing}

### 1. Inform Your Attorney Immediately

Send your lawyer a copy of the PV within 24 hours. They may want to:
- Reference it in ongoing family court proceedings
- File a *requête* (application) for emergency measures under Article 1253ter/4 of the Judicial Code
- Contact the Prosecutor directly

### 2. Log the PV in Your Case File

Record:
- PV number
- Date and time of filing
- Officer name and badge number
- Police zone
- Brief summary of what was documented
- Any evidence submitted

### 3. Follow Up on the Prosecutor's Decision

The *Procureur du Roi / Procureur des Konings* will decide to:
- **Prosecute** (*poursuivre / vervolgen*) — criminal proceedings begin
- **Mediate** (*médiation pénale / bemiddeling in strafzaken*) — an alternative resolution
- **Dismiss** (*classer sans suite / seponeren*) — no action taken

Under the Franchimont Law, you have the right to be informed of this decision. If dismissed, you can pursue a **civil party constitution** (*constitution de partie civile / burgerlijke partijstelling*) before an investigating judge to force investigation.

### 4. Prepare for the Next Exchange

Use each police report to refine your approach:
- Arrive early to exchange points
- Have your phone ready to document (discreetly)
- Bring a witness
- Have the PV number ready in case of a repeat incident

## Special Situations {#special-situations}

### Cross-Border Cases (Belgium + Another Country)

If the other parent has taken the child across Belgian borders:
1. File immediately with local police AND contact:
   - **Belgian Central Authority:** SPF Justice, Service de coopération internationale civile
   - **Child Focus:** 116 000 (European missing children hotline)
   - **Federal Police missing persons unit**
2. The [Hague Convention on International Child Abduction (1980)](https://www.hcch.net/en/instruments/conventions/full-text/?cid=24) applies if both countries are signatories
3. Belgium's implementing legislation: **Law of August 10, 1998**

### When Police Refuse to Take Your Complaint

This happens more often than it should. Your options:
1. Ask for the *commissaire* (station commander)
2. File directly with the *Procureur du Roi* via registered letter
3. Contact the *Comité P* (Standing Committee for Police Oversight): [www.comitep.be](https://www.comitep.be)
4. Contact the *Médiateur fédéral / Federale Ombudsman*
5. Have your attorney send a formal letter to the police zone chief

### Filing in a Language You Don't Speak

Belgium has three official languages (French, Dutch, German). You have the right to file in your language:
- In **Brussels**: French or Dutch
- In **Flanders**: Dutch (French available upon request)
- In **Wallonia**: French (German in the *Communauté germanophone*)
- You may request an **interpreter** — this is your legal right under the **Law on the Use of Languages in Judicial Matters (1935)**

### Emergency Situations (Immediate Danger)

If a child is in immediate danger:
- Call **112** (European emergency number) or **101** (Belgian police emergency)
- The police can intervene immediately under **Article 1253ter/4 of the Judicial Code** (urgent measures)
- A *juge de la jeunesse / jeugdrechter* (youth court judge) can issue emergency protection orders

## Frequently Asked Questions {#faqs}

**Q: Does filing a police report automatically start criminal proceedings?**
No. The *Procureur du Roi* has prosecutorial discretion (*opportuniteitsbeginsel / principe d'opportunité*). They decide whether to prosecute, mediate, or dismiss. However, your report is **always recorded** and can be referenced in family court.

**Q: Can I file a police report in English?**
Belgian police are not obligated to take reports in English. You may bring your own interpreter, or request one through the police. For best results, prepare your written summary in the local official language (French/Dutch/German).

**Q: How much does it cost to file a police report?**
Nothing. Filing a police complaint in Belgium is **completely free.**

**Q: Will the other parent know I filed a police report?**
If the Prosecutor decides to prosecute or mediate, the other parent will be notified. If the case is dismissed, they may not be formally informed — but it remains on file.

**Q: Can I file a police report if I don't have a court order yet?**
Yes, but it's less effective. Without a court order, police may categorize it as a "family dispute" rather than a custody violation. If no order exists and you share parental authority, both parents technically have equal rights to the child's residence.

**Q: How many police reports should I file before going to court?**
There's no magic number, but Belgian family lawyers generally advise that **3-5 documented violations** over a period of time establish a clear pattern. Quality of documentation matters more than quantity.

**Q: Can a police report be used against me?**
Yes, if it's found to be exaggerated, retaliatory, or frivolous. Belgian judges will view a pattern of unfounded complaints as evidence of parental conflict instigation.

**Q: What if the police officer tells me to handle it through my lawyer?**
You have an absolute right to file a formal complaint. Politely insist. If refused, escalate to the station commander, the Prosecutor's office, or the *Comité P.*

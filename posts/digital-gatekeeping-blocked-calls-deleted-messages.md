Digital gatekeeping is the quietest form of interference. No shouting, no scene at the exchange. Just a phone that rings out.

One missed call means nothing. Forty missed calls on scheduled contact nights is a pattern.

Most parents lose this evidence because they record feelings, not facts. This guide shows you how to record facts.

It is written for any parent whose court-ordered calls, video chats or messages are being blocked, ignored or deleted.

## Why Digital Gatekeeping Is Hard to Prove

Every single incident has an innocent explanation. The battery died. The child was asleep. The Wi-Fi dropped.

Courts accept that once. They struggle to accept it every Tuesday for six months. Your job is to make the six-month view impossible to ignore.

## What Counts as Evidence

- Call logs showing date, time and duration of every attempt
- Screenshots of "blocked" or "not delivered" indicators
- Messages sent with no reply, including read receipts
- The court order or agreement setting the contact schedule
- Any written excuse given afterwards

## Step-by-Step: Building the Record

### Step 1: Anchor Everything to the Order
Write down exactly what contact the order grants: days, times, method. Every entry is measured against this baseline.

### Step 2: Log Every Attempt the Same Day
Record date, time, method and outcome. Same-day notes carry far more weight than reconstructed ones.

### Step 3: Export, Don't Only Screenshot
Screenshots can be questioned. Export the full call history and message thread from the phone as the source file, then keep screenshots as supporting views.

### Step 4: Record Explanations Neutrally
If the other parent gives a reason, log it word for word. Do not argue with it in your notes.

### Step 5: Review Monthly
Count scheduled contacts versus completed contacts. A simple percentage is the most persuasive number in the file.

> ⚠️ Common Mistake
> Sending ten angry messages after a missed call. It turns your evidence of gatekeeping into evidence of hostility.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

- Deleting threads to "keep the phone clean"
- Only logging the worst weeks
- Mixing emotional commentary into the log
- Relying on memory for dates

## Advanced Strategies

> 🧠 Mental Model
> Think like a statistician, not a victim. The court is asking one question: how often did ordered contact actually happen?

Cross-reference missed calls with other events: school holidays, new partners, court hearing dates. Gatekeeping that spikes before hearings is a pattern worth showing.

> 💡 Pro Insight
> Keep the same contact method and time every week. Consistency on your side removes every excuse on theirs.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is a call log enough on its own?**
It is strong, but stronger alongside the court order and a written log explaining each entry.

**Should I record calls?**
Recording laws differ by country and state. Check the rules where you and the child live before recording anything.

**What if the child refuses the call?**
Log it exactly as it happened. A child refusing on the other parent's phone, in their home, is still relevant context.

**How far back should I go?**
Start from the date the current contact order began.

**Do deleted messages still count?**
Yes, if you kept your copy. Your sent messages and delivery status remain evidence.

## Final Takeaway

Silence is not nothing. Logged consistently against the court order, it becomes a measurable pattern.

Facts over feelings. Record every attempt, every time.

## The Problem: Therapists as Unwitting Allies

Imagine this scenario: A child tells their therapist, "I'm afraid of my dad." The therapist, trained to believe the child, validates the fear and recommends reducing contact with the father. The therapist writes a letter to the court supporting this recommendation.

What the therapist doesn't know — because they never asked — is that the child's fear was **installed by the mother** through months of coaching, and the father has zero history of abuse.

The therapist just became the alienating parent's most powerful weapon.

This is not a hypothetical. Dr. Richard Warshak (2015) documented how therapists who treat alienation cases without proper training routinely make the problem worse [¹]. They don't intend harm — but intent doesn't matter when a child loses a parent because a professional validated a manufactured narrative.

This problem isn't limited to the United States. In Belgium, where the practice of psychology is regulated by the **Commission of Psychologists (ComPsy)** under a binding Code of Deontology [²], similar patterns emerge in Family Court proceedings. Belgian law firm Keyser Advocaten (2024) documented how parental alienation is increasingly recognized as "emotional abuse" in Belgian family courts, yet therapists who contribute to it face almost no consequences [³].

> ⚠️ **Common Mistake**: Assuming all therapists understand parental alienation. Most graduate programs in psychology and social work include **zero hours** of training on alienation dynamics. A therapist can be excellent at treating depression and completely incompetent at navigating high-conflict custody.

## How Good Therapists Go Wrong

The therapeutic errors in alienation cases follow predictable patterns across jurisdictions — from Los Angeles family courts to the Belgian *Familierechtbank*.

### 1. The "Believe the Child" Trap

Therapists are trained — correctly — to take children's statements seriously. But in alienation cases, "believing the child" without investigating context means believing the **alienating parent's script** delivered through the child's mouth.

A 9-year-old who says "Dad scares me" may genuinely feel fear — but that fear was manufactured through:
- Repeated exposure to the alienating parent's anxiety
- Coached language that mimics genuine distress
- Withdrawal of affection when the child expresses positive feelings about the targeted parent

A trained alienation-aware therapist explores **the origin of the fear**, not just its existence. The APA's Guidelines for Child Custody Evaluations (2022) specifically require evaluators to consider "multiple hypotheses" when assessing children's statements [⁴].

### 2. The "Individual Therapy" Error

When a therapist treats only the child (or only the child and the alienating parent), they receive a one-sided narrative. They never see:
- How the child behaves with the targeted parent (often warmly, when the alienating parent isn't present)
- The alienating parent's coaching behaviors
- The contradiction between the child's stated fear and their actual comfort level

Dr. Janet Johnston (2003) established that **family systems observation** is essential in alienation cases [⁵]. Individual therapy alone is not just insufficient — it's contraindicated.

### 3. The "Protective Parent" Assumption

Some therapists assume that the parent raising the alarm must be protecting the child. They don't consider that the "protective" parent may be the one causing harm through alienation. This assumption turns the therapist into an advocate rather than a clinician.

The Psychiatric Times treatment guidelines (2020) specifically warn against this pattern, noting that therapists must maintain "a position of neutrality" and avoid becoming "an ally of one parent against the other" [⁶].

## The Three Types of Harmful Therapeutic Intervention

### Type 1: Passive Harm (Uninformed Validation)

The therapist doesn't understand alienation dynamics and inadvertently validates the alienating parent's narrative by:
- Recommending reduced contact based on the child's coached statements
- Writing court letters that support the alienating parent's position
- Failing to explore whether the child's rejection is organic or manufactured

**Accountability level**: Professional negligence. The therapist failed to meet the standard of care by not educating themselves on alienation dynamics in a custody context.

### Type 2: Active Harm (Biased Practice)

The therapist aligns with one parent and actively works against the other by:
- Refusing to include the targeted parent in treatment
- Making custody recommendations without proper forensic evaluation
- Coaching the child's testimony (sometimes unknowingly)
- Providing diagnoses of the targeted parent without ever evaluating them

**Accountability level**: Ethics violation. In the US, most licensing boards prohibit this. In Belgium, this directly violates Article 6 of the Code of Deontology, which mandates objectivity and impartiality [²].

The California case *Geffner v. Board of Psychology* (2024) demonstrates real consequences: the Board of Psychology **revoked** a psychologist's license for evaluating children without the father's consent, failing to consult existing therapists, and making recommendations beyond the scope of the evaluation [⁷].

### Type 3: Systemic Harm (Institutional Complicity)

The therapeutic system itself perpetuates the alienation through:
- Intake processes that only include one parent
- Agencies that don't require collateral information from both parents
- Court-appointed therapists who defer to the parent with primary custody
- Insurance systems that only authorize individual (not family) therapy

**Accountability level**: Systemic reform needed. This is bigger than individual therapists.

## Belgium's Code of Deontology: A Model for Accountability

Belgium offers a unique lens for examining therapist accountability because it has one of Europe's most structured regulatory frameworks for psychologists.

### What Is Deontology?

**Deontology** (from the Greek *deon*, meaning "duty") is Belgium's legally binding ethical code for all registered psychologists. Unlike the APA's voluntary ethics code in the US, Belgium's Code of Deontology has been **law since May 26, 2014**, and is enforced by the Commission of Psychologists (ComPsy), an independent federal body [²].

Every psychologist practicing in Belgium must be registered with ComPsy — there are over 18,000 registered as of 2024 [⁸]. The Code establishes binding rules covering:

- **Competence** (Article 3): Psychologists must practice within their area of competence and seek additional training when needed
- **Objectivity** (Article 6): Psychologists must maintain impartiality and avoid bias
- **Informed Consent** (Article 8): Clients must be fully informed about the nature and limits of the therapeutic relationship
- **Confidentiality** (Article 10): With specific exceptions for child protection
- **Dual Relationships** (Article 12): Psychologists must avoid conflicts of interest

### The Disciplinary Mechanism

ComPsy operates **two disciplinary panels** (Dutch-speaking and French-speaking) that can investigate complaints against psychologists [⁹]. The process allows any person — including a parent who believes a psychologist has acted with bias in a custody case — to file a formal complaint.

Sanctions range from:
- Formal warnings
- Mandatory additional training
- Temporary suspension
- **Permanent removal from the register** (effectively ending the psychologist's career in Belgium)

> 💡 **Pro Insight**: Belgium's deontological framework is more enforceable than the US system because registration with ComPsy is **legally required** to practice. In the US, a psychologist can lose their license in one state and simply move to another. In Belgium, removal from the national register is final.

### Where Belgium Falls Short

Despite the strong regulatory framework, Belgian family law attorney firm Wanted Law (2026) notes a critical gap: Belgian legislation does **not formally define parental alienation** as a legal concept [¹⁰]. The Belgian Civil Code (Articles 374 and 387ter) provides for enforcement of custody arrangements and even astreinte (penalty payments) for non-compliance, but does not specifically address the role therapists play in enabling alienation.

Belgian attorney Sarah Van den Berghe has documented how courts increasingly recognize alienation patterns, but the lack of a statutory definition means therapists who contribute to alienation rarely face deontological complaints specifically framed around alienation dynamics [¹¹].

## What Belgian Law Says (and Doesn't Say)

### The Legal Framework

Belgian family law operates under several key provisions relevant to therapist accountability:

**Article 374 of the Belgian Civil Code**: Establishes that both parents retain parental authority (*ouderlijk gezag*) after separation. A therapist who systematically excludes one parent from a child's treatment may be facilitating a violation of this right [¹⁰].

**Article 387ter of the Belgian Civil Code**: Allows courts to impose enforcement measures when a parent's rights are being obstructed. This can include ordering a change of therapist if the current therapist is contributing to the obstruction [¹⁰].

**The Chambers Family Law 2025 Belgium Guide** — authored by Tiberghien lawyers Larissa De Wulf, Carolyn Vanthienen, and Alexandra Goossens — confirms that Belgian courts can appoint experts and order psychological evaluations, but notes the system relies heavily on the quality and impartiality of these professionals [¹²].

### The European Dimension

Belgium is also bound by European instruments, including:

- **The UN Convention on the Rights of the Child** (ratified by Belgium): Article 9 establishes a child's right to maintain contact with both parents [¹³]
- **The European Court of Human Rights**: Has ruled in multiple cases that failure to enforce contact rights constitutes a violation of Article 8 (right to family life) [¹⁴]
- The **Kinderrechtencoalitie Vlaanderen** (Children's Rights Coalition Flanders) and **Liga voor Mensenrechten** have actively litigated children's rights issues before the Belgian *Raad van State* and the CJEU [¹⁵]

### The Council of Europe's Position

The Council of Europe's publication *The Best Interests of the Child — A Dialogue Between Theory and Practice* emphasizes that professionals working with children in custody disputes must be trained to recognize manipulation and coached statements [¹⁶]. A therapist who lacks this training and makes recommendations that sever a child's relationship with a parent may be acting contrary to the child's best interests as defined under European law.

## US Laws Therapists May Be Breaking

While the US lacks Belgium's unified deontological system, therapists who contribute to parental alienation may be violating multiple state and federal provisions:

### 1. State Licensing Laws

Every US state has a licensing board for psychologists, social workers, and marriage/family therapists. Common violations in alienation cases include:

- **Practicing outside competence**: Treating alienation cases without training (violates most state practice acts)
- **Failure to obtain informed consent**: Not disclosing limitations of the therapeutic role
- **Improper forensic opinions**: Making custody recommendations without proper forensic authority
- **Diagnosing without evaluation**: Offering diagnoses of a parent the therapist has never met

The California Board of Behavioral Sciences (BBS) explicitly warns that therapists in custody cases must "adhere to the statutes and regulations governing the practices" and that board complaints can result in formal discipline [¹⁷].

### 2. Mandatory Reporting Failures

Under federal law (CAPTA) and state mandatory reporting statutes, therapists are **required** to report suspected child abuse [¹⁸]. The APA has recognized parental alienation as a form of "family violence" [¹⁹]. If alienation constitutes psychological abuse — as a growing body of research suggests — then a therapist who witnesses alienating behavior and fails to report it may be violating mandatory reporting laws.

### 3. Professional Malpractice (Tort Liability)

To establish therapeutic malpractice, you generally need to prove:

1. **Duty**: The therapist had a professional duty of care
2. **Breach**: The therapist deviated from the accepted standard of practice
3. **Causation**: The breach directly caused harm
4. **Damages**: Measurable harm resulted (lost custody time, emotional distress, financial costs)

HPSO/CNA's case study on inappropriate court testimony in custody disputes documents a real malpractice claim against a licensed professional counselor who provided court testimony exceeding their competence, resulting in a significant settlement [²⁰].

### 4. The Geffner Precedent (California, 2024)

In *Geffner v. Board of Psychology* (2024), the California Court of Appeal upheld the Board of Psychology's **revocation** of Dr. Robert Geffner's license. The violations included [⁷]:
- Evaluating children without the father's consent
- Failing to consult the children's existing therapist
- Making custody and visitation recommendations beyond the scope of the suicide risk evaluation he was retained to perform

This case establishes that **license revocation is a real consequence** for psychologists who overstep their role in custody cases.

### 5. State-Specific Parental Alienation Provisions

A growing number of US states are codifying protections:
- **Massachusetts**: Courts evaluate alienation under the "best interests of the child" standard and can modify custody when alienation is proven [²¹]
- **Multiple states**: Have provisions for filing licensing board complaints specifically related to custody evaluation misconduct [²²]

> 💡 **Pro Insight**: CPH Insurance's analysis of complaints against child custody evaluators shows that one of the most common complaint categories is "favoring the other parent and failing to adequately perform the evaluation" [²²]. Filing a board complaint is free and creates a paper trail — even if the complaint doesn't result in formal discipline.

## Real Consequences: Case Studies

### Case 1: The Validation Letter (California)

A therapist wrote a letter to family court stating that a child exhibited "significant anxiety" in connection with visits to the father and recommended suspension of visitation. The father had no abuse history. An independent forensic evaluation later determined the child's anxiety was the result of the mother's coaching. By the time the evaluation was complete, the father had lost 18 months of contact.

**Outcome**: The therapist faced no consequences. The father spent $47,000 in legal fees to restore contact.

### Case 2: License Revocation (*Geffner v. Board of Psychology*, 2024)

A psychologist evaluated children for suicide risk without the father's consent, failed to consult the children's existing therapist, and made custody recommendations beyond the scope of the evaluation. The Board of Psychology revoked his license.

**Outcome**: The California Court of Appeal upheld the revocation, establishing precedent that psychologists who exceed their forensic authority face real career consequences [⁷].

### Case 3: The Reunification Sabotage

A court ordered reunification therapy for an alienated child and targeted parent. The court-appointed therapist conducted sessions in a way that reinforced the child's rejection rather than facilitating reconnection.

**Outcome**: The targeted parent requested a new therapist. The court agreed after reviewing session notes that showed the therapist was validating the child's alienated stance rather than working toward reunification.

### Case 4: The Malpractice Settlement (HPSO Case Study)

A licensed professional counselor provided inappropriate court testimony in a custody dispute, making diagnostic claims about a parent they had never evaluated. The case resulted in a malpractice settlement.

**Outcome**: The case study is now used as a training example for risk management in professional liability [²⁰].

## What the Ethics Codes Say

### American Psychological Association (APA)

**Guideline 10.02**: Psychologists should not provide therapy and conduct forensic examinations for the same individual [²³].

**Guideline 9.01**: Bases for assessments — psychologists must base their opinions on "information and techniques sufficient to substantiate their findings" [²³].

The **2022 APA Guidelines for Child Custody Evaluations** specifically require evaluators to consider multiple hypotheses and avoid confirmation bias [⁴].

### National Association of Social Workers (NASW)

**Standard 1.01**: Social workers' primary responsibility is to promote the well-being of clients — including the children whose access to a parent they may be inadvertently restricting [²⁴].

### American Association for Marriage and Family Therapy (AAMFT)

**Principle III**: Marriage and family therapists "do not use their professional relationships with clients to further their own interests" [²⁵].

### Belgium: ComPsy Code of Deontology

**Article 3** (Competence): Psychologists must practice within their area of training [²].

**Article 6** (Objectivity): Psychologists must maintain impartiality [²].

**Article 12** (Dual Relationships): Psychologists must avoid conflicts of interest, including serving as both therapist and forensic evaluator [²].

All four codes — APA, NASW, AAMFT, and ComPsy — explicitly prohibit the kinds of interventions that commonly occur in alienation cases when therapists are untrained in high-conflict dynamics.

## How to Document Therapeutic Harm

If you believe a therapist is making your alienation case worse, document systematically:

### Step 1: Request Records
You have a legal right to your child's therapy records. In the US, both parents generally have access unless a court order says otherwise. In Belgium, the Code of Deontology governs access under Article 10 [²].

### Step 2: Identify Specific Violations
Look for:
- Recommendations made without evaluating you
- Diagnoses of you without direct assessment
- Session notes that show one-sided information gathering
- Letters to the court that exceed the therapist's role

### Step 3: Get an Expert Review
Have a forensic psychologist review the records and provide an opinion on whether the therapist's conduct met the standard of care. Baker (2007) provides a comprehensive framework for identifying alienation-specific therapeutic failures [²⁶].

### Step 4: Preserve Everything
Save all correspondence, session summaries, and court filings. Use AntiAlienate's evidence system to create a timestamped, tamper-evident record.

## Filing a Board Complaint: US and Belgium

### In the United States

The process varies by state but generally follows these steps:

1. **Identify the correct board** — psychology (state board of psychology), social work (state board of social work), or MFT (board of behavioral sciences)
2. **Obtain the complaint form** — usually available online. California's BBS provides a specific process for complaints arising from custody cases [¹⁷]
3. **Write a factual narrative** — not emotional, not accusatory. State what happened, when, and what standard was violated
4. **Attach supporting documentation** — records, correspondence, court filings
5. **Submit and follow up** — boards typically acknowledge receipt within 30 days

### In Belgium

The process through ComPsy:

1. **Verify the psychologist is registered** at [compsy.be](https://www.compsy.be/en_GB) [⁸]
2. **Access the complaint form** — available in Dutch and French at [compsy.be/complaint](https://www.compsy.be/en_GB/complaint) [⁹]
3. **Identify the specific deontological articles violated** — most custody-related complaints will reference Articles 3, 6, and/or 12
4. **Submit to the appropriate disciplinary panel** — Dutch-speaking or French-speaking
5. **The disciplinary panel investigates** — the psychologist has the right to review the full complaint file as part of their defense [⁹]

> ⚠️ **Common Mistake**: Filing a complaint as retaliation during an active custody case. Courts in both the US and Belgium notice this, and it can backfire. File because genuine harm occurred, and time the filing strategically with your attorney's guidance.

## Finding the Right Therapist

Not all therapists are harmful. The right therapist can be transformative. Look for:

- **Specific training in parental alienation** (ask directly)
- **Experience with high-conflict custody cases**
- **Willingness to include both parents in the treatment process**
- **Familiarity with forensic standards** (they know their limits)
- **No court letter writing** (ethical therapists decline to write court letters because it compromises the therapeutic relationship)

Red flags:
- They've never heard of parental alienation
- They refuse to consider that a child's rejection might be coached
- They make custody recommendations without forensic training
- They only want to hear from one parent

In Belgium, verify registration with ComPsy [⁸] and ask specifically about training in *ouderverstoting* (the Dutch term for parental alienation) or alienation parentale (French).

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I sue my child's therapist?
Potentially, yes. Therapeutic malpractice claims in custody cases have been successful, though they're complex. The HPSO/CNA case study demonstrates that malpractice claims against counselors in custody contexts do result in settlements [²⁰]. You'll need an attorney who specializes in professional liability and expert testimony from another therapist.

### Can I file a complaint in Belgium?
Yes. Any person can file a complaint with the Commission of Psychologists (ComPsy) against a registered psychologist who has breached the Code of Deontology [⁹]. The process is free and can result in sanctions up to permanent removal from the register.

### What if the therapist was court-appointed?
Court-appointed therapists have the same ethical obligations as private therapists. However, they may have qualified immunity in some US jurisdictions. A licensing board complaint is often more effective than a lawsuit in these cases. In Belgium, court-appointed experts are still subject to the Code of Deontology [²].

### How do I bring up alienation without seeming paranoid?
Frame it clinically: "I'm concerned about the possibility of enmeshment and coached statements. I'd like the therapist to evaluate the child's statements in the context of the family system, not just take them at face value." Use the clinical language.

### What if the therapist is helping my ex but not court-ordered?
You may not have standing to file a complaint about a therapist who isn't treating your child. However, if that therapist is writing court letters or making recommendations about custody, they've entered the forensic arena and are subject to forensic standards — under APA Guidelines [⁴] and ComPsy Article 6 [²] alike.

### Are there therapists who specialize in recognizing alienation?
Yes. Look for practitioners certified by the Parental Alienation Study Group (PASG), or those trained in Warshak's Family Bridges program [²⁷]. In Belgium, the *Familierechtbank* can appoint specialized experts, and organizations like SOS Ouderverstoting can provide referrals.

### What is "deontology" and why should I care?
Deontology is the legally binding ethical framework for psychologists in Belgium, in force since 2014 [²]. Unlike voluntary ethics codes in many countries, violations of Belgium's Code of Deontology can result in **legally enforceable sanctions** including permanent removal from practice. If you're dealing with a psychologist in Belgium who is contributing to alienation, the deontological complaint process is one of the most powerful tools available to you.

## Final Takeaway

Therapists occupy a position of extraordinary trust in custody cases. When they wield that trust responsibly — with training, objectivity, and systems-level thinking — they can be the difference between a child losing a parent and a family healing.

When they wield it irresponsibly — through ignorance, bias, or misplaced advocacy — they become the alienating parent's most credentialed accomplice.

The frameworks for accountability exist. Belgium's Code of Deontology proves that binding, enforceable standards for psychologists are not only possible but practical. The *Geffner* ruling proves that US licensing boards will act when the evidence is clear. The APA's 2022 custody evaluation guidelines prove that the profession itself recognizes the problem.

The answer to the title question is yes. Therapists should be held accountable. Not because they are the enemy, but because the stakes are too high for incompetence to go unchecked.

Your child deserves a therapist who serves the truth — not a narrative.

# When a Child Refuses Contact: Why "Pace" Needs Structure

You've heard it from therapists, evaluators, and judges: *"We need to go at the child's pace."*

It sounds compassionate. It sounds child-centered. But in cases of parental alienation, it is often the single most destructive recommendation a professional can make.

## The Problem With 'Pace' {#the-problem}

When a professional says "go at the child's pace," they typically mean: *wait until the child is ready.* But here's the clinical reality:

- **Children in loyalty conflicts are never 'ready'** — because readiness requires the aligned parent to stop reinforcing rejection
- **Avoidance reduces anxiety short-term but increases it long-term** — this is basic exposure therapy science (Foa & Kozak, 1986)
- **'Pace' without a timeline is indistinguishable from 'never'** — and the aligned parent knows this

💡 **Pro Insight**: In Dr. Richard Warshak's research, children who were given unlimited 'pace' showed *increased* resistance over time, not decreased. Avoidance became their coping mechanism, and the longer it continued, the harder reunification became.

## Why Children Refuse Contact {#why-children-refuse}

Child resistance to a parent falls into three categories:

### 1. Realistic Estrangement
The child has genuine reasons based on direct experience (abuse, neglect, trauma). **This is real and must be taken seriously.**

### 2. Affinity-Based Preference
The child prefers one parent's household (more permissive, closer to friends). **Normal developmental behavior.**

### 3. Alienation-Driven Rejection
The child's rejection is:
- Disproportionate to any real experience
- Uses adult language and reasoning
- Extends to the entire extended family
- Accompanied by 'borrowed scenarios' (stories that didn't happen to them)
- Intensifies around transitions

⚠️ **Common Mistake**: Treating category 3 the same as category 1. When alienation-driven rejection is treated as genuine fear, the intervention (avoidance/pace) *reinforces the alienation.*

## The Difference Between Fear and Loyalty Conflict {#fear-vs-loyalty}

This distinction is critical:

| Feature | Genuine Fear | Loyalty Conflict |
|---------|-------------|------------------|
| Onset | Gradual, tied to events | Sudden, tied to parental conflict |
| Specificity | Specific fears ('Dad yelled at me') | Global rejection ('I hate everything about Dad') |
| Ambivalence | Child shows some positive feelings | Child shows zero ambivalence |
| Extended family | Child still likes grandparents | Child rejects entire family |
| Language | Age-appropriate | Adult phrasing, legal terminology |
| Recovery | Improves with safety | Improves when aligned parent isn't present |

🧠 **Mental Model**: A child with genuine fear wants the scary behavior to stop. A child in a loyalty conflict wants the *parent* to disappear. The first is specific and proportionate. The second is absolute and performative.

## What Structured Pacing Actually Looks Like {#structured-pacing}

Structured pacing is NOT 'wait and see.' It is a planned, incremental reintroduction with:

1. **Clear timelines** — 'We will have a 30-minute supervised visit by [date]'
2. **Progressive exposure** — Phone → video → brief in-person → extended
3. **Accountability for both parents** — The aligned parent must actively support contact
4. **Professional oversight** — A reunification therapist, not the child's individual therapist
5. **Consequences for non-compliance** — If the aligned parent sabotages, the court acts

### A Sample 8-Week Structure:

- **Week 1-2**: Parent sends 3 brief, warm texts per week (no response required)
- **Week 3-4**: Two 15-minute video calls (therapist present)
- **Week 5-6**: One 1-hour in-person visit in neutral location
- **Week 7-8**: One 3-hour activity-based visit
- **Week 9+**: Gradual expansion toward normal parenting time

💡 **Pro Insight**: The key is that the schedule is *non-negotiable.* The child's feelings are validated ('I know this feels hard'), but the contact happens regardless. This is identical to how exposure therapy works for anxiety — you don't wait until the anxious person 'feels ready' to face their fear.

## The Research Behind Structured Reintroduction {#research}

Multiple studies support structured over unstructured approaches:

- **Warshak (2010)**: Family Bridges program showed 90%+ success rate with structured weekend workshops, compared to near-zero success with 'pace' approaches
- **Fidler & Bala (2010)**: Identified that court-ordered gradual reintroduction with therapeutic support produced better outcomes than voluntary contact
- **Sullivan & Kelly (2001)**: Found that children's stated preferences in alienation cases were unreliable indicators of their actual needs
- **Gottlieb (2012)**: Demonstrated that 'respecting the child's wishes' in alienation cases led to permanent relationship loss in 85% of cases

## What Parents Can Do Right Now {#what-to-do}

1. **Document every recommendation** — If a professional says 'go at the child's pace,' ask them to put it in writing with a specific timeline
2. **Request a reunification specialist** — Not the child's therapist, not a general family therapist
3. **Present the research** — Bring Warshak, Fidler, and Gottlieb to your attorney
4. **Keep reaching out** — Even if ignored, consistent warm messages create a record of effort
5. **Don't argue with the child** — Validate feelings while maintaining the relationship: 'I understand you're upset. I love you and I'm not going anywhere.'

## Frequently Asked Questions {#faqs}

### Isn't forcing contact traumatic for the child?
Research consistently shows that *loss of a parent* is more traumatic than the temporary discomfort of reintroduction. The discomfort typically resolves within days when the aligned parent supports the process.

### What if the child is a teenager?
Teens deserve more input, but their stated preferences still need professional evaluation. A 14-year-old who uses legal language and shows zero ambivalence is not making an autonomous choice.

### How do I know if it's alienation vs. genuine fear?
A comprehensive custody evaluation by a forensic psychologist using validated instruments (like the PPAT or Baker's 17 strategies) is the gold standard.

### What if my therapist disagrees with structured approaches?
Many individual therapists are trained in client-centered models that prioritize the child's stated wishes. This is appropriate for most situations but contraindicated in alienation. Seek a specialist.

## Final Takeaway {#final-takeaway}

'Going at the child's pace' is not inherently wrong — it's *incomplete.* Without structure, timelines, accountability, and professional oversight, 'pace' becomes a euphemism for permanent parental loss.

The question is never *whether* to respect a child's feelings. It's whether those feelings should be the *sole* decision-maker — or whether adults have a responsibility to structure the path back to a healthy two-parent relationship.

The research is clear: structure works. Unstructured 'pace' doesn't.

**Your child needs you. Don't let 'pace' become 'never.'**

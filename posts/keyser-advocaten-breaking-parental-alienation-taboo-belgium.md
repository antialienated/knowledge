## Why This Matters

Let's be blunt.

Most family law attorneys **refuse to use the term 'parental alienation'** in their marketing, their case strategy, or their court filings. The reasons vary:

- **Fear of being labeled 'fringe"**: The term has been politicized, particularly in academic circles that associate it with [discredited theories](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/basics/parental-alienation) or custody disputes where abuse allegations are genuine.
- **Judicial skepticism**: Many judges remain unfamiliar with or hostile to the concept, making it risky to build a case around it.
- **Complexity avoidance**: Alienation cases are long, emotionally draining, and difficult to prove. Many attorneys prefer cleaner, faster matters.

So when a **respected, established firm** — one that serves high-net-worth individuals, entrepreneurs, and physicians — publishes under their masthead that alienation is real, is abuse, and is being systematically ignored by professionals, that's not just an article.

That's a professional risk. And it's exactly the kind of risk that starts to shift the Overton window.

## The Professional Silence Problem

The taboo around parental alienation isn't accidental. It's structural.

### How the Silence Gets Built

1. **Academic gatekeeping**: A small but vocal group of researchers argue that 'parental alienation' is a pseudo-concept used to silence abuse victims. Their papers get cited. Their frameworks get adopted by training programs.

2. **Professional self-preservation**: Therapists, social workers, and custody evaluators who acknowledge alienation risk professional complaints, peer criticism, and even lawsuits from the alienating parent.

3. **Judicial inertia**: Judges who haven't encountered alienation cases (or who encountered poorly argued ones) develop skepticism. That skepticism gets communicated to attorneys. Attorneys stop raising it.

4. **Media framing**: Mainstream media tends to frame alienation through the lens of 'he said / she said' custody disputes, which flattens the reality into a false equivalence.

The result? **Parents experiencing genuine alienation** — documented, provable, pattern-evident alienation — are told by their own attorneys to "not use that word."

Keyser Advocaten said the word. On purpose. In public.

## The 'Weaponized Term' Argument — Deconstructed

Here's the elephant in the room.

Critics argue that 'parental alienation' is a weaponized term — a label abusive parents use to deflect from genuine abuse allegations. And they're not entirely wrong. **The term has been misused.** Some parents do invoke it manipulatively.

But here's what the critics miss:

### The False Dichotomy

The argument is structured as: *"Either alienation is real OR it's a weapon used by abusers."*

That's a false binary. **Both can be true simultaneously.**

- Some parents weaponize the term. ✓
- Some parents are genuinely alienated. ✓
- Both populations deserve justice. ✓

Dismissing all alienation claims because some are fraudulent is like dismissing all abuse allegations because some are false. [No serious professional would do the latter](https://www.childwelfare.gov/). No serious professional would do the latter — yet many do the former.

### What Keyser Gets Right

Keyser Advocaten navigates this by focusing on **documented behaviors**, not labels:

- They identify specific, observable patterns (negative influence, isolation, resistance)
- They build cases on evidence, not emotion
- They coordinate with mental health professionals who can distinguish genuine alienation from estrangement

This is exactly the framework that courts need — and it's exactly why firms like Keyser matter.

⚠️ **Common Mistake**: Don't lead with the label 'parental alienation' in court. Lead with the documented behaviors. Let the evidence speak the diagnosis. Keyser's approach models this perfectly.

## Why Belgium Matters for European Precedent

Belgium's legal system has several features that make it a potential bellwether for alienation jurisprudence across Europe:

1. **Civil law tradition**: Belgium follows the Napoleonic code tradition, which influences France, the Netherlands, Luxembourg, and beyond. Legal developments in Belgian family courts can ripple across these jurisdictions.

2. **EU institutional proximity**: With the European Court of Human Rights and EU institutions nearby, Belgian legal developments often feed into broader European human rights frameworks.

3. **ECHR Article 8**: The right to family life under the [European Convention](https://www.echr.coe.int/documents/d/echr/Convention_ENG) is increasingly being invoked in alienation cases. Belgian attorneys are well-positioned to build these arguments.

4. **Bilingual/trilingual complexity**: Belgium's Dutch/French/German legal landscape means attorneys must already navigate cross-cultural family dynamics — exactly the skill set needed for alienation cases.

## Belgium's Equal Custody Revolution {#equal-custody-revolution}

In 2006, Belgium passed a law that sent shockwaves through European family law. The **Act of 18 July 2006** amended the Belgian Civil Code to establish a **presumption of equal alternating residence** (*gelijkmatig verdeeld verblijf* / *hébergement égalitaire*) for children of separated parents.

This was radical. At a time when most European countries still defaulted to primary residence with one parent (overwhelmingly the mother), Belgium declared that **both parents are equally important** — and that courts must start from that premise.

The law did not mandate 50/50 custody in every case. But it required judges to **consider equal residence first** and provide specific justification for any deviation. The burden shifted: instead of a parent having to prove they deserved equal time, the court had to explain why equal time was not appropriate.

### Why 2006 Matters

Belgium's law was not born in a vacuum. It emerged from decades of advocacy by fathers' rights organizations, psychological research on the importance of dual-parent involvement, and a growing recognition that the 'tender years doctrine' — the assumption that young children belong with their mother — was neither scientific nor just.

The 2006 reform placed Belgium at the forefront of a global movement. Countries like Sweden, Denmark, and later Australia would follow similar paths. But Belgium was among the first to **codify** the presumption into statute.

> 🧠 **Mental Model**: Think of Belgium's 2006 law as a constitutional amendment for family law. It didn't just change a rule — it changed the **default assumption**. Every custody case in Belgium now starts from the premise that the child needs both parents equally. Deviation requires justification.


## The Legal Framework: What the Law Actually Says {#legal-framework}

Belgium's family law framework for custody and parental authority is built on several key provisions:

### Belgian Civil Code, Article 374 §2

The cornerstone. This article establishes that when parents cannot agree on custody arrangements, the court must:

1. **Examine the possibility of equal alternating residence first**
2. Only deviate from equal residence if it is not in the child's best interest
3. Provide **specific, reasoned justification** for any deviation

This is not a suggestion — it is a legal obligation on judges.

### Parental Authority (Ouderlijk Gezag / Autorité Parentale)

Belgian law distinguishes between:

- **Joint parental authority**: Both parents share decision-making on education, health, religion, and other major life decisions. This is the **default** and continues after separation regardless of residence arrangements.
- **Exclusive parental authority**: Granted only in exceptional circumstances where joint authority is demonstrably harmful to the child.

Critically, **a parent who obstructs the other parent's exercise of parental authority is violating the law** — regardless of the residence arrangement.

### The Youth Protection Act

Belgium's youth protection system operates through two community-level services:

- **SAJ** (Service d'Aide à la Jeunesse) in the French-speaking community
- **CBJ** (Comité Bijzondere Jeugdzorg) in the Flemish community

These services can investigate family situations, provide support, and recommend interventions. However, they have **no direct enforcement power** — they can only advise the court.

> ⚠️ **Common Mistake**: Assuming that involving SAJ/CBJ will solve the problem. These services are investigative and supportive, not coercive. If the alienating parent refuses to cooperate with their recommendations, you still need court enforcement.


## The Enforcement Arsenal {#enforcement-arsenal}

When a Belgian parent violates a custody order, the other parent has several legal tools available:

### 1. Return to the Family Court (Tribunal de la Famille)

The first step is always to file for enforcement with the family court that issued the original order. The court can:

- Reaffirm the existing order with a warning
- Modify the order to account for the violation
- Impose an **astreinte** (penalty payment)
- In severe cases, **reverse custody**

### 2. Criminal Complaint (Non-Représentation d'Enfant)

Belgian criminal law (**Article 432 of the Penal Code**) criminalizes the deliberate withholding of a child from the parent entitled to custody or contact. This is known as *non-représentation d'enfant* (failure to present the child). Penalties can include:

- **Imprisonment** of 8 days to 1 year
- **Fines** of €26 to €10,000

In practice, criminal prosecution for non-representation is rare but not unheard of — and the mere filing of a criminal complaint can change the dynamics significantly.

### 3. Enforcement via Bailiff (Huissier de Justice)

A parent can engage a bailiff to **physically enforce** a custody order. The bailiff can go to the alienating parent's residence and demand the child be handed over. If the parent refuses, the bailiff documents the refusal — creating powerful evidence for subsequent court proceedings.

### 4. Contempt Proceedings

Belgian courts can hold a parent in contempt for repeated violations of court orders. While less commonly used than astreinte, contempt proceedings carry the weight of the court's authority.

> 💡 **Pro Insight**: The most effective strategy in Belgium is a **layered approach**: file for astreinte in family court, simultaneously file a criminal complaint for non-représentation, and engage a bailiff for documented enforcement attempts. This creates a paper trail that is almost impossible for the alienating parent to explain away.


## Landmark Rulings That Changed Everything {#landmark-rulings}

Belgium's legal landscape has been shaped by several pivotal decisions:

### Marckx v. Belgium (ECHR, 1979)
**Application No. 6833/74 | European Court of Human Rights**

The case that started it all. While Marckx dealt specifically with the rights of children born out of wedlock, the ECHR's ruling established a foundational principle: **Article 8 of the European Convention on Human Rights protects the full scope of the parent-child relationship**.

The Court declared that the right to family life is not limited to "traditional" family structures — it extends to every parent-child bond, regardless of the parents' marital status. This reasoning would later become the bedrock of every ECHR ruling on custody enforcement and parental alienation.

**Why it matters for alienation**: Marckx established that Belgium (and every Council of Europe member) has a positive obligation to protect parent-child relationships. When a state allows alienation to proceed unchecked, it violates this obligation.

### Cour de Cassation, 6 March 2025 (C.24.0157.F)
**Belgium's Supreme Court**

Belgium's highest court addressed parent-child relationship interference in the context of custody proceedings in this recent ruling. This decision signals that Belgian jurisprudence is aligning with the ECHR's increasingly firm stance on enforcement failures.

The significance of a Cour de cassation ruling cannot be overstated: it sets binding precedent for all lower Belgian courts. When the Supreme Court recognizes interference with the parent-child relationship as a factor in custody determinations, it gives family court judges explicit authority to act decisively.

### L.D. v. Poland (ECHR, 2025)
**Application No. 12119/14 | Judgment: 13 February 2025**

While this case was against Poland, its implications for Belgium are direct and binding. The ECHR ruled that:

- States have a **positive obligation** to enforce contact orders
- The **passage of time causes irremediable harm** to the parent-child bond
- A state's failure to act constitutes a **violation of Article 8**

This ruling applies to Belgium with full force. Belgian lawyers can — and should — cite L.D. v. Poland in domestic proceedings.

For a detailed analysis of this ruling and its implications across all 46 Council of Europe member states, see our guide: [**Article 8: The Legal Weapon Every Alienated Parent in Europe Needs to Know**](/blog/echr-article-8-parental-alienation-weapon).

### Strand Lobben and Others v. Norway (ECHR, 2019)
**Grand Chamber | Application No. 37283/13**

The Grand Chamber's highest-authority ruling established that *"the mutual enjoyment by parent and child of each other's company constitutes a fundamental element of family life."* This language has been cited in virtually every subsequent ECHR custody case and is directly applicable in Belgian courts.

> ⚠️ **Common Mistake**: Assuming ECHR case law is merely 'advisory' in Belgian courts. Belgian courts are bound by the European Convention, and ECHR interpretations are directly applicable. Failing to cite relevant ECHR jurisprudence in your Belgian proceedings is a strategic error.


## The Astreinte System: Europe's Most Powerful Enforcement Tool {#astreinte-system}

The **astreinte** (Dutch: *dwangsom*) is Belgium's signature enforcement mechanism — and arguably the most powerful tool available to alienated parents anywhere in Europe.

### How It Works

An astreinte is a **court-ordered penalty payment** imposed on a party who fails to comply with a court order. In custody cases:

1. The court issues or reaffirms a custody/contact order
2. The court attaches an astreinte: a specific amount payable **for each day (or each violation)** that the order is not complied with
3. If the parent violates the order, the penalties accrue automatically
4. The owed amount can be collected through standard debt enforcement mechanisms

### Typical Amounts

Belgian family courts typically impose astreinte in the range of:

- **€50–€100/day** for initial violations
- **€200–€500/day** for repeated or egregious violations
- **€1,000+/day** in extreme cases involving international abduction or complete contact denial

These amounts accumulate. A parent who denies contact for 30 days at €200/day faces a **€6,000 debt** — and that debt is enforceable through seizure of bank accounts, wages, and property.

### Why Astreinte Is So Effective

Unlike criminal penalties (which require prosecution and carry a high burden of proof), astreinte operates within the civil system:

- **Lower burden of proof**: The violation simply needs to be documented
- **Automatic accrual**: No need to return to court for each violation
- **Financial pressure**: Creates an escalating economic incentive to comply
- **Cumulative evidence**: If astreinte fails to achieve compliance, the documented pattern of violation becomes powerful evidence for custody reversal

> 💡 **Pro Insight**: Request the astreinte **at the same time as the initial custody order**, not after a violation occurs. Belgian judges can attach astreinte preventively. This sends a clear signal from day one that violations will have immediate financial consequences.


## The Belgian Debate: Science vs. Politics {#belgian-debate}

Belgium is not immune to the global controversy surrounding parental alienation. A significant study published in 2024 by **Jeunesse et Droit** — *"L'aliénation parentale: étude du concept et des pratiques en Belgique francophone"* — examined how French-speaking Belgian courts and practitioners handle alienation claims.

The study revealed a deep divide:

### The Practitioners' View
Family court judges, mediators, and forensic psychologists who deal with high-conflict cases daily widely recognize alienation dynamics as a real and measurable phenomenon. They observe:

- Children who suddenly and irrationally reject a previously loved parent
- Custodial parents who systematically obstruct contact
- Patterns that align precisely with the clinical literature (Gardner, Kelly & Johnston, Warshak, Bernet)

### The Critics' View
Organizations like **CODE** (Coordination des ONG pour les droits de l'enfant) urge caution, arguing that:

- The concept can be misused to dismiss legitimate safety concerns
- Children's expressed preferences should be respected
- The focus should be on the child's well-being rather than parental rights

### The Resolution

The ECHR's jurisprudence cuts through this debate with surgical precision: **regardless of what you call the phenomenon**, when a parent's court-ordered contact rights are being systematically obstructed and the state fails to act, it is a violation of Article 8.

You do not need to convince a Belgian judge that "parental alienation" exists as a syndrome. You need to demonstrate that:

1. A contact order exists
2. The order is being violated
3. The state has not taken adequate measures to enforce it
4. The passage of time is causing irremediable harm to the parent-child bond

This framework bypasses the ideological debate entirely. It is rooted in human rights law, not psychology. And it is binding on every Belgian court.

## Brussels II Ter: The EU's Cross-Border Shield {#brussels-ii-ter}

As an EU member state, Belgium is bound by **Regulation (EU) 2019/1111** — commonly known as **Brussels II ter** (replacing Brussels IIa). This regulation is critical for cases involving:

- Parents living in different EU member states
- Children who have been wrongfully removed to or retained in another EU country
- Cross-border enforcement of custody and contact orders

### Key Provisions

- **Mutual recognition**: Custody orders issued in one EU member state are directly enforceable in all others
- **Abolition of exequatur**: For certain orders, no separate enforcement proceedings are needed in the other member state
- **Right of the child to be heard**: Courts must provide the child an opportunity to express their views
- **Anti-abduction measures**: Strengthened return mechanisms and time limits

### Why This Matters for Belgian Cases

Belgium's position at the heart of the EU — home to the European Commission, European Parliament, and European Council — makes it a frequent venue for cross-border custody disputes involving EU officials, diplomats, and international families.

Brussels II ter ensures that a Belgian custody order cannot simply be ignored by a parent who flees to another EU country. The order follows the child across borders.


## How to Build Your Case in Belgium {#build-your-case}

### Step 1: Document Every Violation
Use a systematic evidence platform to log every denied visit, every unreturned call, every manipulated exchange. Timestamps, screenshots, and witness statements are essential.

### Step 2: Request Astreinte Early
Do not wait for violations to accumulate. Request astreinte attachment to your initial custody order as a preventive measure.

### Step 3: Engage a Bailiff
For each violation, consider engaging a *huissier de justice* to document the non-compliance officially. Bailiff reports carry significant weight in Belgian courts.

### Step 4: File Criminal Complaints
For serious violations, file a criminal complaint for *non-représentation d'enfant* (Article 432 Penal Code). Even if prosecution does not follow, the complaint is on record.

### Step 5: Request Psychological Evaluation
Ask the court to appoint a forensic psychologist (*expert judiciaire*) to evaluate the family dynamics. Specifically request an expert trained in high-conflict custody and alienation dynamics.

### Step 6: Cite ECHR Jurisprudence
In every filing, cite **L.D. v. Poland**, **Strand Lobben**, and **Marckx v. Belgium**. Remind the court of Belgium's positive obligations under Article 8.

### Step 7: Escalate to Custody Reversal
If astreinte and other measures fail, petition for **custody reversal**. Belgian courts have the authority to transfer primary residence to the non-alienating parent — and ECHR case law supports this as an appropriate remedy when other measures have failed.

> ⚠️ **Common Mistake**: Giving up after one failed enforcement attempt. Belgian courts respond to **patterns**. Each documented violation, each bailiff report, each unpaid astreinte builds the case for decisive action. Persistence is not optional — it is the strategy.

## Frequently Asked Questions {#faq}

### Is parental alienation recognized in Belgian law?
Belgian law does not use the specific term 'parental alienation.' However, Belgian courts recognize and act on the underlying behaviors: obstruction of contact, manipulation of the child, and interference with parental authority. The legal framework addresses the conduct, not the label.

### How much does an astreinte typically cost the violating parent?
Belgian family courts typically impose €50–€500 per day of violation. For a parent who denies contact for an entire month at €200/day, that's €6,000 — collectible through wage garnishment and asset seizure.

### Can I get criminal charges filed against the alienating parent?
Yes. Article 432 of the Belgian Penal Code criminalizes non-représentation d'enfant (failure to present the child) with penalties of up to 1 year imprisonment and €10,000 in fines. Criminal prosecution is rare but available.

### Does Belgium default to 50/50 custody?
Belgium presumes equal alternating residence as the starting point. However, the court can deviate from this if it determines that equal residence is not in the child's best interest. The key difference from most countries is that the **burden is on the party opposing equal custody** to justify deviation.

### Can I use ECHR case law in Belgian courts?
Absolutely. ECHR jurisprudence is directly applicable in Belgian courts. You should cite relevant cases (L.D. v. Poland, Strand Lobben, Marckx v. Belgium) in all enforcement filings.

### What if the other parent flees to another EU country?
Brussels II ter (EU Regulation 2019/1111) ensures that Belgian custody orders are directly enforceable across all EU member states. Contact a lawyer specializing in international family law immediately.

### How long does a custody enforcement case take in Belgium?
Family court proceedings typically take 2–6 months. However, urgent measures (*mesures urgentes*) can be obtained within days to weeks. If domestic remedies are exhausted, an ECHR application typically takes 3–7 years.

Belgium proves that having the right law is not enough. You must have the will to enforce it.

The 2006 equal custody reform was visionary. The astreinte system is powerful. The criminal code provides teeth. And the ECHR stands as the ultimate guarantor. But none of these tools work if you don't use them — systematically, persistently, and strategically.

Belgium has given alienated parents one of the strongest legal arsenals in Europe. The tragedy is that most parents don't know it exists. Now you do.

Document everything. Request astreinte from day one. File criminal complaints when warranted. Cite the ECHR at every turn. And if Belgium's courts fail you, take your case to Strasbourg.

# How to Talk to a Child Before Transitions (A Simple Script)

The car is packed. It's time to go to the other parent's house. Your child is stiffening, crying, or shutting down.

What you say in the next 60 seconds will determine whether this transition builds the bridge — or burns it.

## Why Transitions Are the Flashpoint {#why-flashpoint}

Transitions are when:
- The child's loyalty conflict is at maximum intensity
- The aligned parent's subtle (or overt) signals are strongest
- The child's anxiety peaks because they're crossing between 'worlds'
- Alienating messages land hardest: *'Call me if you need me'* = *'You might not be safe'*

Research by Pruett & Barker (2009) found that **the quality of transitions predicted child adjustment better than the custody schedule itself.** The 'how' matters more than the 'how much.'

---

## What NOT to Say {#what-not-to-say}

Before we give you the script, here's what to eliminate:

❌ *"Do you want to go?"* — This creates a false choice and puts the decision on the child

❌ *"I'll miss you so much"* — This guilts the child for leaving

❌ *"Call me if anything happens"* — This implies danger

❌ *"Be good for your [mom/dad]"* — This implies the other parent is an authority figure to be endured, not enjoyed

❌ *"I wish you didn't have to go"* — This tells the child that transitions are bad

❌ *[Crying, clinging, visible distress]* — Children mirror parental emotions. Your distress becomes their distress.

## The Transition Script (By Age Group) {#the-script}

### Ages 3-6: The Adventure Frame

*"Guess what? You get to go to [Mommy/Daddy]'s house today! I bet you're going to [activity they enjoy there]. That sounds fun, right? I love you. Have an awesome time. I'll see you on [specific day]."*

**Key elements**: Excitement, specificity about return, positive association with the other household.

### Ages 7-10: The Confidence Frame

*"Hey, it's time to head to [Mom/Dad]'s. I know transitions can feel weird sometimes — that's totally normal. You're going to have a good time. [Mom/Dad] is looking forward to seeing you. I'll see you [day]. Love you."*

**Key elements**: Normalize the weirdness, express confidence in the other parent, specific return date.

### Ages 11-14: The Respect Frame

*"Time to head out. I know you've got [homework/plans/activity] this week at [Mom/Dad]'s. Text me if you need anything for school. Have a good week. See you [day]."*

**Key elements**: Treat them as capable, focus on practical continuity, low-pressure check-in option.

### Ages 15+: The Low-Key Frame

*"Alright, you're all set? Cool. See you [day]. Love you."*

**Key elements**: Don't over-process. Teens need normalcy, not scripts. Keep it brief and warm.

## The Science Behind the Script {#the-science}

Every element of this script is built on research:

- **Positive framing** (Pruett, 2009): Children who hear positive statements about the other parent show lower transition anxiety
- **Specific return dates** (Wallerstein, 2000): Knowing when they'll return reduces the child's fear of permanent separation
- **Confidence signaling** (Siegel & Bryson, 2012): When the sending parent is calm and confident, the child's nervous system down-regulates
- **No false choices** (Warshak, 2010): Asking 'do you want to go?' creates a loyalty test. Statements create safety.

🧠 **Mental Model**: You are the air traffic controller. Your job is to make the 'landing' at the other parent's house smooth. You don't ask the plane if it wants to land. You guide it in.

## What to Do When It Doesn't Work {#when-it-doesnt-work}

Sometimes the child will still resist. Here's the escalation:

1. **Acknowledge**: *'I can see this is hard for you right now.'*
2. **Validate without joining**: *'Your feelings are real. AND we're still going.'*
3. **Redirect**: *'Let's pick out a book/toy to bring with you.'*
4. **Physical transition**: Walk them to the car. Movement breaks the freeze.
5. **Brief, warm goodbye**: Don't linger. Lingering increases distress.

If the child physically refuses:
- Follow your refusal protocol
- Document the refusal
- Contact your attorney/parenting coordinator
- DO NOT engage in extended negotiations with the child

## For the Receiving Parent {#receiving-parent}

The receiving parent has a script too:

*"Hey! I'm really glad you're here. I was thinking we could [specific plan]. Want to help me [task]?"*

**Key principles**:
- **Activity, not interrogation**: Don't ask about the other household
- **Immediate engagement**: Give the child something to do within 5 minutes
- **Low pressure**: Don't force conversation. Let them settle.
- **Parallel play for younger kids**: Be in the same space doing something together, no forced eye contact

💡 **Pro Insight**: The first 15 minutes after arrival predict the rest of the visit. If you can get through those 15 minutes with a calm activity, the child's nervous system will regulate and the visit will go well.

## Frequently Asked Questions {#faqs}

### What if my ex badmouths me right before the transition?
You can't control what happens at the other house. You *can* control the tone you set when the child arrives. A warm, calm greeting is the best antidote to whatever was said.

### Should I ask my child how the transition went?
Not immediately. Let them settle. If they want to talk, they will. Asking 'how was the drop-off?' puts them right back into the loyalty conflict.

### What if my child is fine leaving my ex but refuses to leave me?
This is a common asymmetry in alienation cases. The child is anxious about leaving the aligned parent because they fear that parent's reaction, not because they don't want to see you.

### Can I record transitions?
Check your local laws. In many jurisdictions, recording in public spaces is legal. Having a record of smooth transitions (or sabotaged ones) can be powerful evidence.

## Final Takeaway {#final-takeaway}

Transitions aren't just logistics. They're the moment where a child learns whether it's safe to love both parents.

The words you use — and the words you don't use — tell your child everything they need to know about whether you support their relationship with their other parent.

**60 seconds. The right words. A bridge instead of a wall.**
